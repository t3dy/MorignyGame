Summarize each chapter of this book in an audiobook style focusing only on what we learn about John of Morigny's biography and magical texts. A subsequent prompt will cover the history of scholarly discovery and analysis of Morigny and the associated manuscript tradition.
Claire Fanger, Rewriting Magic: An Exegesis of the Visionary Autobiography of a Fourteenth-Century French Monk
Chapter One, “Like Stones of Fire: I Encounter the Book of Visions”

Claire Fanger presents John of Morigny as a highly educated Benedictine monk and priest attached to the Abbey of Morigny near Étampes. He possessed an advanced degree in canon law from the University of Orléans and occupied a position of some institutional responsibility within his monastery. His own writings reveal a man deeply immersed in biblical exegesis, liturgy, theology, the liberal arts, and the learned magical literature of his period. The central text through which Fanger reconstructs his intellectual and spiritual life is the Liber florum celestis doctrine, the “Flowers of Heavenly Teaching,” whose first major component is the autobiographical Book of Visions.

The biography that emerges from the Book of Visions is fundamentally a story of John's pursuit of knowledge through forbidden arts, followed by a prolonged process of recognition, repentance, and substitution. John first became interested in necromantic knowledge. While still a relatively young monk, he obtained from a cleric a book containing what he describes as “nefarious things of the necromantic art.” He copied as much of it as he could. His interest then became more deliberate, and he sought advice from a Lombard medical expert, Jacob of Bologna. Jacob directed him toward the ars notoria, telling him that through it he would discover not only the truth concerning the particular magical knowledge he sought but “all forms of knowledge.” Thus John presents his first encounter with the ars notoria as a movement from one magical technology of knowledge toward another, supposedly more comprehensive one.

This distinction between the two forms of magic becomes fundamental to John's autobiography. He does not describe himself as simply wandering indiscriminately among occult practices. Instead, his magical career has identifiable phases. Necromancy initially represents a direct and obviously dangerous form of magical knowledge, while the ars notoria appears to offer something much more attractive: access to the liberal arts, philosophy, theology, and ultimately the comprehensive knowledge that Adam was imagined to have possessed before the Fall.

John consequently regarded the ars notoria as holy when he first encountered it. Its prayers were Christian in vocabulary and extraordinarily elevated in style. They invoked God and angels, employed biblical language, and promised access to knowledge through prayer, fasting, contemplation, and ritual practice. The danger, as John later came to understand it, was precisely this appearance of sanctity. He characterizes the art as something beautiful and apparently rational whose underlying operation is corrupt. Fanger emphasizes that John therefore regarded the ars notoria as more subtle and deceptive than ordinary necromancy: its evil was concealed beneath a devotional exterior.

The most important new evidence about John's actual use of the ars notoria comes from his descriptions of dream incubation. John repeatedly describes a practical sequence. He fasts, waits until twilight, recites a prayer from the ars notoria, extinguishes his light, goes to bed, and experiences a dream or ecstatic vision. The visions are not simply predetermined images supplied by the magical book. They are often strange, unstable, and ambiguous, and John subsequently interprets them. He sometimes chooses a particular ars notoria prayer because of the information he wants to obtain. On another occasion, he explicitly uses a prayer associated with memory because he wants to understand the meaning of earlier frightening dreams.

The resulting visions are strikingly concrete. In one, after fasting and using an ars nova prayer, John finds himself at night beside his mother's house in Autruy. A gigantic demonic hand casts five enormous fingers as shadows upon a wall. When John turns toward the moon, he sees the monstrous spirit producing the shadow. Yet the apparition then changes: the enormous demonic figure descends into the field in the guise of a simply dressed holy man. John even experiences a moment of uncertainty and regrets having called the figure a malign spirit before waking. The episode establishes an important characteristic of John's visionary experience: supernatural appearances are not always cleanly divided into unmistakable good and evil.

Another vision occurs after John has been using the ars notoria for a considerable period. After reciting the “Sign of Grace” prayer and going to bed, he sees what he interprets as the Trinity: two men are visible, while a third presence is sensed rather than seen. The first figure, whom John initially takes to be the Father, complains that John has not prayed long enough; the second identifies him as “Father.” The figure whom John understands as Christ then promises that he will receive a vision eight days later. Again, the experience combines explicit ritual technique with ambiguous visionary perception.

John's experimentation also extended beyond himself. He taught the ars notoria to his younger sister Bridget, partly because she wanted to learn to read. She had never seen letters, yet after John taught her through the art she learned to read and write within approximately six months and was able to sing an Alleluia publicly. But she subsequently experienced terrifying demonic nightmares. A spirit would press upon her in bed, threaten her, and prevent her from speaking or moving. John eventually instructed her to renounce the ars notoria before God and the Virgin. The demon withdrew, and Bridget followed his instructions the next day. John later says that she became capable of defeating and trampling the demon whenever it appeared.

John also taught the art to his Cistercian friend John of Fontainejean. Fontainejean subsequently sought a visionary interpretation of the mysterious names and words of the ars notoria. His dream placed him in his monastery among books of the art. He encountered a monk reading the Psalter and discovered, through the dream's allegory, that what he sought was not in the Psalter. John interprets this as divine confirmation that the ars notoria does not belong to divine law and is not pleasing to God.

Fanger's central characterization of the Book of Visions is therefore that it is not a straightforward diary of magical experiments. It is a retrospective confession in which John represents his own intellectual and spiritual formation. He openly admits that he regarded the ars notoria as holy, continued using it after receiving warnings, and eventually moved into other forms of magic. The purpose of exposing his own errors is to establish how divine grace intervened in his life. His autobiography therefore becomes the experiential foundation for the replacement of the ars notoria with the Liber florum.

The crucial magical-textual fact established by this chapter is that John did not merely condemn the ars notoria and abandon its objectives. He appropriated its basic project. The Liber florum would become a Christian replacement for it: a new ritual technology for obtaining divine knowledge through prayers, visions, and eventually figures. John's strategy was not simply to destroy the magical book but to “plunder” its useful material and reconstitute it within a Christian devotional framework.

Chapter Two, “A Mysticism of Signs and Things: The Ars Notoria and the Sacraments”

The second chapter explains why the ars notoria could initially have appeared legitimate to someone like John. Its importance lies in the fact that John was not looking for magic merely to produce spectacular effects. He was looking for a disciplined method of acquiring knowledge and transforming himself.

Fanger argues that John's understanding of the ars notoria must be placed within a medieval intellectual environment in which learning, prayer, sacramental practice, and spiritual transformation could belong to a single process. Hugh of Saint Victor provides an especially important model. In Hugh's thought, creation itself is a kind of divine book, and the Incarnation constitutes another “writing” of divine Wisdom. Human learning therefore means learning to read the divine order embodied in Scripture, the world, Christ, and the sacraments.

This framework helps explain John's attraction to the ars notoria. The liberal arts were not simply secular academic subjects. They could function as stages in the restoration of the intellect damaged by the Fall. Grammar, rhetoric, dialectic, arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy trained the human faculties needed for higher knowledge. The ars notoria promised an extraordinary acceleration of this process through prayer and ritual. To John, therefore, its appeal was partly devotional: it seemed to offer a method by which intellectual learning could simultaneously become spiritual purification.

The problem was that the ars notoria presented itself as sacred while, in John's eventual interpretation, contaminating the sacred. Fanger uses the biblical and exegetical concept of sacrilege to explain John's distinction. Necromancy was dangerous because it involved consciously illicit relations with demons. The ars notoria was in some respects worse because it mixed sacred forms with a forbidden operation. Its Christian prayers and elevated rhetoric made its corruption harder to recognize. John calls this a form of “sacrilegious” prayer.

John consequently constructs the Liber florum as an antidote. His replacement preserves the ars notoria's basic concern with knowledge but changes the source, ritual logic, and theological orientation. The prayers are directed toward God, Christ, Mary, and the angels. The desired knowledge remains extraordinarily broad, extending from the liberal arts to philosophy and theology. But knowledge is now explicitly subordinated to purification, virtue, salvation, and divine grace.

The chapter therefore establishes an important principle for understanding John's magical texts: the Liber florum is not an unrelated devotional book written after John abandoned magic. It is a transformation of the magical knowledge system that had formed him. Its structure, objectives, ritual sequencing, and visionary techniques preserve substantial elements of the ars notoria, while John reinterprets them as Christian and sacramental.

Chapter Three, “Penance: The Sacrament of the Middle of Life”

Chapter Three follows John's conversion away from the ars notoria through the sacrament and practice of penance. Fanger's concern here is specifically with the way John's magical biography becomes a penitential narrative.

John explicitly states that everything written in the book belongs under the sacrament of penance. The Liber florum is therefore simultaneously an account of sin and a ritual program for overcoming it. The Seven Prayers recall John's own experiences of conversion, while the Thirty Prayers repeatedly ask for purification and reform. The book as a whole becomes both meditation on penance and a practical means of performing it.

The decisive visions occur as John attempts to abandon the ars notoria. In one, the eighth vision, John is thrown down a staircase by an angel and seized by a devil. He realizes that his continued use of the ars notoria has brought him into danger. He escapes when he resolves to find a confessor, confess his sins, and renounce them. He then encounters a bishop, confesses, and enters a church where he sees the Virgin Mary and John the Evangelist. The vision culminates around the Eucharistic altar, where the Evangelist appears in the animal-headed form associated with the apocalyptic living creatures.

This vision is subsequently transformed into ritual instructions. John's fourth prayer reproduces elements of the visionary experience as visual meditation. The operator is told to imagine entering a church, seeing Mary and John the Evangelist, being embraced, and receiving their attention. John's private visionary experience is thus converted into a reproducible devotional procedure. The Liber florum turns autobiography into ritual technology.

The ninth vision produces the decisive break. John has been troubled by numerous horrible visions and uses an ars notoria prayer for memory in order to understand them. A malign spirit appears, which John drives away with a sword. A tall man in an ashen robe then enters and asks John to interpret a vision. When John protests that the man ought to interpret the vision for him, the figure tells him to stop meddling in such matters. The man then asks John to hear his confession. John attempts to absolve him, but instead of ordinary words of absolution, the figure causes John to utter the words “King of kings, prince of princes and lord of lords, man, God, son of Mary born of a virgin.”

John subsequently realizes that the mysterious penitent is Christ. The revelation gives him the certainty he needs: the ars notoria is entirely malign, God does not want him to operate through it, and his own use of it has displeased God. He therefore abandons the art completely, confesses, and repents.

The important textual consequence is that John's experience becomes embedded in Prayer One, “O Rex regum.” The prayer expands the words of the vision into a meditation on Christ's Incarnation, Passion, redemption, Wisdom, and mercy. The personal episode is thus rewritten as cosmic theology. John's private magical error becomes part of the universal Christian history of Fall, Incarnation, Passion, and restoration.

The chapter's most important biographical complication is that John's conversion is not instantaneous in the simple sense. Even after receiving a decisive warning, he initially puts the ars notoria aside “somewhat, but not entirely.” His sister Bridget is actually more decisive in renouncing the practice. John therefore presents himself not as a magically purified saint but as a sinner whose conversion occurs through repeated correction, confession, and divine intervention.

The chapter also clarifies the relationship between John's priesthood and his magical experience. His visions repeatedly place him in situations involving confession, absolution, Mass, the altar, and priestly authority. The magical quest for knowledge is being transformed into a sacramental quest in which the purification of the operator is a prerequisite for legitimate revelation.

Chapter Four, “Errors of Intellect, Errors of Will: I Encounter the Book of Figures”

The fourth chapter moves from the ars notoria to the next major stage of John's magical career: the Book of Figures. John began developing this component around 1308, several years after his rejection of the ars notoria. The Figures were intended to accompany the prayers as visual and meditative instruments, constituting what John sometimes calls a “Second Procedure.”

The Old Compilation was much more elaborate than the surviving New Compilation. Fanger reconstructs an original system of approximately ninety-one figures. These included seven figures of the Virgin, seven planetary figures, twelve zodiacal figures, figures associated with the twelve houses, and numerous additional configurations. John provided enough information about the first fourteen figures to permit their reconstruction.

The Old Compilation was not simply a collection of pictures. It was an integrated ritual system of prayer, figure, visualization, and personal inscription. John instructed operators to incorporate their own names into the figures together with the name of Mary and the “property” associated with each figure. The letters were interwoven into cross-shaped configurations. This was not decorative personalization. The operator's identity was conceptually joined to Mary, the relevant cosmic or intellectual power, and the Passion of Christ.

One surviving figure provides an especially clear example. It consists of a pentangle containing a cross and the monogram of Jesus Christ, with the names “Maria” and “amica” woven through it. The accompanying words constitute a prayer asking Mary for mercy, hearing, instruction, illumination, counsel, and assistance. John calls this the general figure for an experiment intended to recover a lost visionary capacity. It was to be used after penitential purification, so the figure did not mechanically force a vision. Rather, it served as an aid to the purified soul.

Another important figure associated the Tetragrammaton with the four intellectual faculties of intellect, memory, eloquence, and perseverance. The letters of the divine name were positioned in the four directions, while the figure incorporated corresponding intellectual attributes. John interpreted this kind of design as a spiritual machine for restoring the soul and leading it toward paradise.

The planetary figures were particularly unusual. John describes seven anthropomorphic images: a winged cherub, crowned female figures, Christ in different forms, the Virgin in both old and young aspects, God as an old man, and Christ as a crowned king or armed soldier. Fanger argues that these figures resemble the variable anthropomorphic imagery found in medieval image magic, although John transforms the imagery into an unmistakably Christian vocabulary.

The figures therefore represent the most visibly magical component of John's work. They employ planetary correspondences, geometric forms, crosses, circles, Hebrew letters, divine names, angelic names, and personalized inscriptions. Yet John understands them through Christian devotional and sacramental categories. Their efficacy ultimately depends on God, not on the autonomous power of the diagram.

The chapter ends with the crisis that destroyed the Old Compilation as an operative system. Certain critics whom John calls the “Barking Dogs” attacked the figures as resembling necromantic figures because of their crosses and circles and because they incorporated planetary and daily cycles. John was particularly distressed because he believed the figures had been developed with the Virgin's guidance and confirmation. The attack forced him to reconsider the entire project.

Chapter Five, “Magical Objects of Knowledge: Categorizing the Exceptive Arts”

Chapter Five concentrates on John's relationship with necromantic texts. Fanger makes clear that John's magical reading did not end with the ars notoria. After his decisive rejection of that art, he entered another period in which he read, possessed, and practiced necromantic material.

The most striking evidence is John's work with the “Four Rings of Solomon.” While fashioning the fourth ring, he receives a divine voice repeatedly calling him a fool. This experience finally contributes to his abandonment of magic as a whole. Yet another vision shows that John continued to possess necromantic books afterward.

One vision is especially revealing. At Chartres, John sees a silver image of the Virgin transform into the Virgin herself. Mary tells him simply to worship God and give thanks. John then worries about what would happen if his community discovered that he possessed books of necromancy. Would they conclude that his visions of Mary and his miraculous images were themselves products of necromantic manipulation? He wonders whether he should hide or remove the books. Fanger notes that John never actually tells us what happened to them. Among the books was apparently a nova nigromancia, a work he himself had been composing.

This evidence gives the biography a particularly human dimension. John's repentance does not mean that knowledge acquired through forbidden texts can simply be erased. His books remain physical repositories of intellectual labor, and the knowledge they contain continues to shape his thought. Even when John rejects necromancy, its categories remain embedded within his intellectual system.

Fanger then reconstructs John's conception of the “exceptive arts.” John explicitly places necromancy within a larger category that includes aeromancy, pyromancy, chiromancy, geomancy, geonegia, and hydromancy. These arts are not simply meaningless superstition in his intellectual system. They constitute identifiable branches of knowledge, even though their practical use may be prohibited.

The distinction between knowledge and practice is particularly important. In John's prayers for the exceptive arts, the operator asks to know and understand the arts but explicitly not to perform them. His prayer for necromancy requests theoretical knowledge of practices such as sacrifice, suffumigation, and auscultation while prohibiting their performance. Thus John does not entirely remove magic from the architecture of knowledge. He separates knowing magic from doing magic.

The category of geonegia is especially revealing because John associates it with a group of magical and talismanic books. He names a work on images attributed to Ptolemy, the Book of Talismans of Abel, the Book of Seven Senators, the Book of Twelve Firmaments, and the Book of the Semhemforas. Several of these texts are identifiable with traditions of image magic, planetary magic, divine names, and talismanic operations.

This provides some of the strongest evidence that John's magical library extended beyond the ars notoria. His intellectual world included traditions associated with planetary images, talismans, divine names, Hebrew and Arabic magical texts, and the broader Liber Razielis tradition. Fanger does not claim that every one of these works directly supplied John's figures, but the correspondences suggest that his Book of Figures emerged from a much broader magical environment than the ars notoria alone.

The chapter also identifies a system of correspondences linking liberal arts, mechanical arts, exceptive arts, gifts of the Holy Spirit, planets, and virtues. One figure, for example, links intellect, grammar, weaving, necromancy, patience, and Saturn. The operator's own name is incorporated into the figure. The result is a vast conceptual machine in which intellectual, practical, magical, spiritual, and astrological domains occupy corresponding positions.

This is the deeper significance of John's magical texts. He is not merely collecting spells. He is attempting to integrate every kind of knowledge into a single divine architecture. Even forbidden magic remains conceptually inside the architecture, although its practice is excluded. His problem is therefore not that he has no theory of where magic belongs. His problem is precisely that he possesses too much knowledge of its place within the larger system.

Chapter Six, “Visionary Exegesis and Prophecy: Milk and Meat”

The final chapter explains how John reconstructed the meaning of his entire magical career after the condemnation of the Old Book of Figures.

The crisis begins when the Old Figures are attacked. John responds by producing the New Compilation, beginning the work in August 1315 and completing it within a matter of months. The New Compilation radically simplifies the system. Instead of the elaborate collection of planetary, zodiacal, and other figures, it concentrates on seven figures of the Virgin and one apocalyptic image of Christ.

John's revision does not, however, amount to an admission that his earlier work was simply demonic. His fundamental problem is different. He believes that he made an error by acting before receiving the divine instruction he had been promised. In a vision, Mary had told him not yet to move or alter the figures. John nevertheless proceeded. He consequently defines his error as a failure of obedience and discernment. His penance is represented in the text itself: the erroneous instructions remain visible, but corrective glosses are added so that future operators will not follow them.

The flowers and leaves that surround portions of the New Compilation become signs of this penitential process. John calls them a sign of the chastisement of his error and imagines that later operators will encounter them as a lesson in obedience. The physical book thus becomes a record of his spiritual development. His errors are not erased; they are preserved as part of the sacred history of the book.

The central conceptual move of the chapter is John's reinterpretation of the Old and New Compilations through the biblical relationship between the Old and New Testaments. The Old Compilation was genuine but incomplete. The New Compilation does not simply replace a false book with a true one. Instead, the New reveals the meaning of the Old. John's earlier visions contained prophecies that he himself had failed to understand until the later revelation.

This allows John to preserve the divine status of his earlier work while acknowledging its inadequacy. His old figures were not meaningless or wholly evil. They represented an earlier stage of spiritual development. His error becomes analogous to the “childhood” of the Christian soul.

This is where the imagery of milk and meat becomes central. John interprets the earlier phase of his relationship with Mary through the vision in which he sleeps at her breast and drinks her milk. During this period he plays with the infant Christ and freely creates figures. Later, when he has matured, Mary no longer gives him milk but solid food. He must now stretch out his arms and allow Mary to gird him and lead him where he does not choose to go.

The metaphor transforms the entire history of the magical book. The Old Compilation represents spiritual childhood: John is protected by Mary, accompanied by Christ, and allowed considerable freedom in designing his figures. The New Compilation represents maturity: John relinquishes control, receives the forms of the figures through revelation, and submits his own creative will to divine direction.

The figure system itself becomes simpler and more explicitly Christian. The New Compilation's seven Marian figures place the Virgin and child at the center. The old Hebrew letters of the Tetragrammaton are replaced by the four nails of the Passion. The complex letter braids and occult-looking constructions disappear. The imagery becomes concentrated around Mary, Christ, the Passion, flowers, birds, and crosses.

The cross remains the most important sign. John understands it as the hidden source of the figures' efficacy. Even when it is not visibly drawn, it is mystically present. The cross connects the Tetragrammaton, Christ's Passion, the Incarnation, and the restoration of human knowledge. In this interpretation the figures are not autonomous magical machines. They are configurations of the instruments through which human beings participate in redemption.

John's prophetic identity also becomes explicit in this final stage. He claims that dreams can legitimately communicate divine revelation because Scripture itself contains numerous examples of God speaking through dreams. He argues that the moral state of the dreamer and the effects of the dreams provide criteria for discernment. Dreams that arise from a life devoted to celestial contemplation and produce greater virtue can reasonably be regarded as divine rather than demonic.

His opponents had attacked precisely this point. They argued that John's dreams might be demonic illusions, that devotion to the Virgin might constitute idolatry, that the figures might embody tacit demonic pacts, and that uncertain visions should be interpreted negatively. John responds as a canon lawyer. He argues that his practice belongs to a divine covenant rather than a demonic pact, that his contemplative discipline distinguishes his visions from demonic deception, and that God would not systematically abandon those who sincerely seek him.

The chapter therefore ends by making John's entire magical biography into a theory of prophetic learning. His original encounter with the ars notoria, his experiments with necromancy, his terrifying visions, his repentance, his construction of the Old Figures, their condemnation, and his production of the New Figures are all stages in one continuous process of revelation.

The most important point is that John does not understand the New Compilation as a repudiation of his earlier intellectual life. Instead, he reads his entire career retrospectively as providentially ordered. His errors become necessary stages in revelation. His magical books become stages in the development of a Christian technology of knowledge. His mistakes become part of the prophecy they initially obscured. And the book itself becomes a material analogue of his own soul: revised, corrected, wounded, penitential, and ultimately redirected toward God.

Taken together, Fanger's six chapters present John of Morigny as a remarkably unusual medieval magical author: a Benedictine priest and canon lawyer who deliberately pursued forbidden technologies of knowledge, first through necromancy and then through the ars notoria; who experienced both demonic and divine-seeming visions through those practices; who taught the art to his sister and fellow monk; who then transformed his magical experience into the Liber florum; who developed an elaborate system of figures drawing on Christian, astrological, angelological, and image-magical traditions; who was forced by condemnation to revise that system; and who finally interpreted the entire sequence as a divinely guided progression from magical error through penitence toward prophecy.

The resulting Liber florum is consequently not just a prayer book and not just a Christianized ars notoria. In Fanger's reconstruction, it is the textual record of John's changing relationship with magical knowledge itself. Its Book of Visions records the acquisition and rejection of magical knowledge; its Book of Prayers converts that experience into repeatable devotional practice; its Old Book of Figures attempts to give the practice a visual and cosmological architecture; and its New Book of Figures transforms the failure of that architecture into a prophetic lesson about obedience, discernment, spiritual maturity, and divine revelation.

Fanger’s treatment of John is best understood as an attempt to show that he did not operate in a separate “magical” intellectual world. His ritual project is built out of the same theological vocabulary that structured ordinary Benedictine and Latin-Christian life: sacrament, confession, penance, grace, revelation, Scripture, liturgy, Marian devotion, the divine names, the Incarnation, the Eucharist, angelology, prophecy, and the distinction between legitimate and illicit knowledge. What makes John unusual is that he recombines these elements into a technology for acquiring knowledge.

John and sacramental theology

The most important sacramental category for John is penance.

Fanger calls penance the “sacrament of the middle of life.” Baptism initiates Christian life and extreme unction closes it, but penance can be repeated whenever the Christian falls into error. That makes it an ideal structure for John's autobiography, because his story is fundamentally one of repeated error, recognition, confession, partial conversion, renewed error, and further correction.

This is more than a thematic resemblance. John explicitly says that his book is categorized under the sacrament of penance. Fanger consequently reads the entire Liber florum as simultaneously a meditation on penitence and a practical instrument of penitential action. The Seven Prayers repeatedly transform episodes from John's own visionary autobiography into prayers that future operators can perform themselves.

Confession is particularly important because John occupies three positions simultaneously. He is a Christian who confesses, a priest who hears confession, and a canon lawyer trained in the juridical theory governing confession. Fanger therefore sees John's visionary experiences as bringing together the “inner forum” of conscience and the “outer forum” of canon law. Confession is where institutional knowledge becomes interior psychological knowledge.

That helps explain why John's autobiography is so unusually confessional. He is not merely telling us that he once practiced magic. He is constructing a penitential account of how knowledge itself can become sinful, how a person can recognize that error, and how knowledge can subsequently be reconstructed.

The Eucharist belongs to this system as well, although Fanger argues that it is not the primary sacramental focus of the Book of Visions. Eucharistic devotion and confession were increasingly intertwined in later medieval piety, with confession functioning as preparation for communion. John's emphasis on confession therefore participates in a broader sacramental rhythm rather than standing apart from Eucharistic Christianity.

The deeper point is that John's magical problem is also a sacramental problem. The question is not simply, “Is this ritual magical?” It is, “What kind of ritual is capable of mediating divine grace and knowledge?” John gradually tries to make his own book operate through the same economy of divine-human mediation that governs the sacraments.

The Incarnation and Mary as the theological model for John's book

This is one of Fanger's most interesting arguments.

Mary is not simply John's favorite saint or the recipient of his prayers. She provides the theological model for how his book can work.

Mary is the human participant through whom the divine Word becomes incarnate. John correspondingly imagines his book as a vehicle through which divine words become active again in human practice. The Thirty Prayers ask Mary to give efficacy to the words spoken by the operator.

Fanger therefore describes the book as a kind of extension of the Incarnation. Christ is the Word delivered through Mary; John's book is the Word restored to words and enacted by individual operators.

This is why Marian devotion is not decorative. Mary is structurally necessary to John's epistemology.

The Virgin is simultaneously:

the source of the book's authorization,

the object of its prayers,

the mediator of divine knowledge,

the model for human participation in divine action,

and the guarantor that the ritual's efficacy comes from God rather than demons.

This becomes especially important after John's experience with the ars notoria. He wants essentially the same result—rapid access to divine knowledge—but wants to relocate the mechanism from a suspect magical tradition into a Marian and sacramental economy.

The Ars Notoria and the sacraments

Fanger's Chapter Two makes the comparison explicit.

John's vocabulary overlaps substantially with that of the ars notoria. Both use words such as “sacrament,” “mystery,” and “secret.” But John gradually changes what these words mean within his own system.

This is where Fanger's Wittgensteinian “language game” becomes useful. John is not simply stealing magical terminology and putting Christian labels on it. He is participating in a shared theological vocabulary while changing the rules governing its use.

The ars notoria itself already draws extensively upon Christian liturgy and theology. Its prayers contain biblical language, Christian litanies, liturgical formulae, and a theology in which divine Wisdom can communicate knowledge to human beings. Fanger emphasizes that even apparently non-Christian magical texts could draw upon the same Neoplatonic and theological traditions as Christian didactic literature.

Consequently, John's problem is not that magic and theology occupy two completely different universes.

They are already talking about many of the same things.

The disagreement concerns what counts as legitimate participation in the divine economy.

That is why John can regard the ars notoria as initially holy. It looks like a legitimate Christian technology of Wisdom. Its prayers resemble Christian prayers. Its goal—knowledge of Scripture, liberal arts, philosophy, and theology—is itself legitimate. The problem emerges from John's experience that the ritual's apparent divine mediation has been contaminated by demons.

His solution is therefore not to abandon the theological game. It is to rebuild the ritual within what he understands as a legitimate sacramental structure.

Grace and human agency

John's theology also depends upon a delicate distinction between what humans can do and what only God can do.

The operator prepares the conditions: prayer, fasting, confession, meditation, visualization, bodily discipline, liturgical observance, and sometimes sleep.

But the operator does not compel God.

This is particularly important in Fanger's later essay “Divine Dreamwork.” John solicits visions, but a solicited vision can refuse the desired answer. God can say no. The whole point is that visionary practice creates a context for divine response without determining what that response will be.

That is very close to sacramental logic. A sacrament is efficacious because divine grace operates through it, not because the human performer possesses autonomous magical power.

John's ritual system consequently tries to occupy a narrow theological space:

human preparation + divine freedom + divinely authorized mediation = legitimate visionary knowledge.

That is why the Virgin's authorization matters so much. John needs an authoritative divine intermediary to distinguish his practice from the demonic imitation of divine mediation that he believes he encountered in the ars notoria.

Scripture as a rulebook for visionary practice

John also treats Scripture itself as a precedent for dream revelation.

When the Barking Dogs attack his work, he defends visionary knowledge by citing biblical examples: Saul consulting God, Joseph's prophetic dream, Nebuchadnezzar's dream, and the Magi being warned in dreams. His argument is essentially that God has historically chosen dreams as a legitimate means of communicating divine will.

This is important because John is not arguing:

“Magic works because I experienced it.”

He is arguing:

“God communicates through dreams in Scripture; therefore visionary communication cannot in itself be illegitimate.”

Experience supplies the evidence; Scripture supplies the authorization.

This is one of the recurring structures of John's theological language game.

Prophecy

John's understanding of visions also draws upon biblical prophecy.

A vision is not necessarily a prediction in the modern sense. It can reveal God's judgment, give instructions, explain an earlier vision, correct an error, or authorize the continuation of a ritual.

Fanger's 2018 article is particularly useful here because she shows that John participates in a much older Christian tradition in which people deliberately pray for visionary clarification. Benedictine figures such as Elisabeth of Schönau and Rupert of Deutz provide striking precedents.

Thus John's solicited visions are not simply “magic dreams.” They belong simultaneously to:

monastic prayer,

prophetic revelation,

liturgical time,

dream interpretation,

discernment of spirits,

and ritual magic.

That overlap is exactly what makes John historically interesting.

Discernment of spirits

The distinction between divine and demonic revelation is another theological language game that John has to master.

His experiences are deliberately ambiguous. Demons and good angels can appear close together. Christ can appear in unexpected forms. A dream may contain a divine message without God appearing directly.

This is why John's autobiography is not simply a series of supernatural encounters. It is also a record of learning how to interpret them.

He learns that:

appearance does not guarantee identity;

a vision's emotional intensity does not guarantee its divine source;

a ritual can produce visions without necessarily producing legitimate revelation;

and the interpretation of a vision may itself require further prayer and revelation.

That is a sophisticated theology of discernment.

The Old and New Testaments as a model for rewriting

Fanger also sees John's textual revision as sacramental and dispensational.

The Old Compilation and New Compilation are structurally analogous to an Old and New dispensation. The New does not simply destroy the Old; it fulfills, supersedes, and transforms it.

That is why Fanger calls the second half of the book “Restoration.” John's rewriting of the Book of Figures becomes analogous to restoration after rupture.

This is crucial for understanding John's response to the Barking Dogs.

He does not simply conclude:

“My magical figures were condemned, therefore I was wrong.”

Instead, the condemnation becomes part of the process through which the book itself is regenerated.

Theologically, error becomes an occasion for a new dispensation.

That is essentially the same structure as John's penitential autobiography.

John's visionary experiences

Fanger's Book of Visions contains a sequence of experiences, but it is important not to turn them into a modern “vision diary.” Fanger emphasizes that the autobiography is retrospectively constructed and that visions are repeatedly reinterpreted and incorporated into later prayers.

The principal experiences are:

The youthful Chartres vision.

As a young adolescent at Chartres, John experiences a vision involving demonic threat and the Virgin Mary. This becomes the foundational scene for his later religious identity. The Virgin rescues him from the devil and establishes the Marian framework that will govern his subsequent work. Feiss summarizes the episode as John's vision of the devil at about thirteen, from whom Mary rescued him.

The visions surrounding his attraction to magical knowledge.

John's early intellectual hunger is connected to his poverty as a student and his desire for books and knowledge. He encounters necromantic material and subsequently the ars notoria. His later visions reinterpret this period as a dangerous search for knowledge that initially appeared legitimate.

The demonic visionary experiences produced through the ars notoria.

These are among the most important episodes in the autobiography. John encounters disturbing visionary figures, including demons masquerading as religious figures. These experiences gradually undermine his confidence that the ars notoria is holy.

The vision of Mary and John the Evangelist.

Fanger identifies this as Vision 8. John recognizes Mary and John the Evangelist immediately. It is a major Marian encounter and prepares the decisive rejection of the ars notoria.

Vision 9: the decisive rejection of the ars notoria.

This is the crucial vision of the Book of Visions. A mysterious tall, ashen, penitent figure appears. He asks John to hear his confession. John eventually recognizes the encounter as a divine revelation demonstrating that the ars notoria is wholly malign. He renounces it and makes confession.

John then composes the prayer Ave, salve in memory of the vision. The prayer turns the visionary experience into a repeatable devotional exercise: the operator is instructed to imagine entering the church, seeing Mary and John the Evangelist, being embraced by Mary, and conversing with her.

This is a fundamental mechanism of the Liber florum: vision becomes prayer; prayer becomes visualization; visualization becomes ritual procedure.

The post-ars notoria Marian vision at Chartres.

After turning away from the ars notoria and later from necromancy, John experiences the famous vision in which the silver image of Mary at Chartres becomes the Virgin herself. She descends from the altar, takes John's hand, directs him to worship God, and tells him to give thanks. The choir sings the Te Deum.

The vision also raises the practical problem of John's magical books. He worries that observers will attribute the miracle to his magical knowledge rather than to Mary. He asks what he should do with his books. That question becomes one of the central problems of his conversion.

Visions associated with John's necromantic experiments.

These are later than the ars notoria experiences and concern a more explicitly necromantic tradition. Fanger emphasizes that John distinguishes the two categories rather than simply treating the ars notoria as necromancy. His conversion away from the ars notoria occurs at Vision 9; his conversion away from necromancy is treated in Visions 10 and 11.

Vision 10: the transition away from necromancy.

John is engaged in necromantic experimentation, including the Four Rings of Solomon. Divine intervention interrupts his practice.

Vision 11: the Christ/penitent vision.

A figure dressed in red appears, whom John recognizes through the Holy Spirit as Christ. The vision is structured around confession, penance, absolution, and divine justice. John receives words of absolution apparently “as if” he had seen them in a book.

This is one of the clearest examples of Fanger's argument that John's visionary theology and sacramental theology cannot be separated. The vision itself is essentially a sacramental drama.

Bridget's demonic visions.

John's sister Bridget had learned the ars notoria from him. She subsequently experiences a terrifying demonic apparition while in bed. John cannot see the demon himself, but he hears and observes her terror. Ordinary prayers do not immediately end the experience. When John tells her to renounce the ars notoria before the Virgin, the demon withdraws. She subsequently develops power over the demon.

This episode is crucial because it externalizes John's own conversion. He caused Bridget to encounter the same knowledge technology that endangered him. Her experience becomes evidence for his own theological interpretation.

John of Fontainejean's vision.

The final vision in this portion of the autobiography concerns John's Cistercian friend John of Fontainejean, whom Morigny had taught the ars notoria. Fontainejean asks for a dream explaining the mysterious words of the art.

He dreams that he is in his monastery among his books and encounters a monk reading the Psalter. When he asks what he is looking for, he is told that what he seeks is not there. John's interpretation is that the ars notoria is not the Psalter: it does not belong to divine law and is not pleasing to God.

This final episode is significant because John's story has become pedagogical. His experience is no longer merely about his own conversion; it is being used to interpret the experience of another operator.

The larger theological pattern

These visions form a remarkably coherent sequence:

innocent encounter with the supernatural;

Marian rescue;

desire for knowledge;

encounter with magical knowledge;

apparent success;

demonic contamination;

discernment;

renunciation;

confession;

penance;

Marian authorization;

new ritual knowledge;

transmission to others;

new visionary experiences;

and eventually textual revision.

That is why Fanger regards the Liber florum as much more than a grimoire. Its ritual technology is inseparable from a theology of conversion.

Here there is an important bibliographical caveat.

Fanger states in Rewriting Magic that she was aware in 2015 of 23 manuscripts containing John's work in whole or in part: one Old Compilation, eighteen containing all or substantial portions of the New Compilation, and four prayer collections containing unattributed sequences derived from John. She explicitly says that she does not give the complete list in Rewriting Magic and directs readers to the manuscript list maintained by Societas Magica and to Introduction B of the Fanger–Watson edition.

The surviving evidence I can retrieve from the attached Fanger material identifies the following witnesses securely:

Graz, Universitätsbibliothek, 680.
This is the crucial manuscript for the early scholarly recovery of John. It contains the long version of the Book of Visions, dramatically expanding what Fanger and Watson initially knew. It revealed the detailed account of John's use of the ars notoria, his friends and family, and the sequence of visions.
Klagenfurt, Studienbibliothek, Cart. 1.
A New Compilation witness associated with the Benedictine monastery of St. Paul in Carinthia. It is particularly significant for demonstrating the monastic transmission and for the “template” type of manuscript in which blank spaces are left for an operator's name.
Klosterneuberg, Stiftsbibliothek, Cod. 950.
Another religious-house witness, associated with an Augustinian canonry. Like Klagenfurt, it demonstrates the use-oriented character of John's book rather than merely scholarly preservation.
Salzburg, Studienbibliothek, M I 24.
Made for use by monks of the Benedictine community of St. Peter in Salzburg. Fanger's book also uses this manuscript photographically, making it one of the more visually important witnesses.
Seitenstetten, Benediktinerstift, Cod. 273.
A Benedictine-house copy and therefore further evidence of the specifically monastic afterlife of John's work.
Vienna, Schottenkloster, MS 140 (61).
Another Benedictine witness. It was one of the three Austrian manuscripts whose discovery in the 1990s transformed Fanger and Watson's understanding of the geographical survival of the work.
Munich, Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, Clm 276.

Bologna, Biblioteca Comunale dell'Archiginnasio, A. 165 (16.b.III.5).
An Italian witness to the New Compilation, demonstrating the considerable geographical range of the work beyond the Benedictine houses of central Europe.
Turin, Biblioteca Nazionale, G.II.25.

Oxford, Bodleian Library, MS Liturg. 160.

Manchester, Chetham's Library, A-4-108.
Another Compilation 3A witness. It may have originated in Italy and is incomplete at the beginning. It is particularly interesting because Fanger identifies it as the only manuscript definitely known to have originated in Britain and as the most recent witness in that group.

Those are the 15 witnesses that can be identified directly from the manuscript appendix reproduced in Fanger's Invoking Angels and the associated material in Rewriting Magic.

I would not invent the remaining eight names from secondary manuscript lists. Fanger explicitly warns that the manuscript count was changing almost annually, and the 23-manuscript statement in Rewriting Magic is not itself accompanied by a complete list. The complete list she refers readers to is her manuscript catalogue, while the definitive treatment is Introduction B, “The Manuscripts and their Users,” in the 2015 Fanger–Watson edition.

That distinction matters because there is already evidence that the count continued changing after 2015: later scholarship has referred to 24 or more witnesses.

So the historically accurate formulation is: Fanger knew 23 witnesses in 2015, but the accessible text of Rewriting Magic does not give us all 23 names, and the manuscript census has continued to change. I can give you a genuinely manuscript-by-manuscript account of all 23, but I would want to work from Introduction B of the 2015 edition rather than manufacture the missing eight from later catalogues.

The important thing about the manuscript corpus as a whole is nevertheless clear. It is not a collection of dead archival copies. Many copies were personalized, with users inserting their own names into the prayers and figures; some were explicitly kept as exemplars for further copying; and several are traceable to Benedictine or other religious communities. Fanger therefore sees the manuscript tradition as evidence of continued ritual use, not merely textual survival.

That makes the sacramental argument especially significant. John's work survived because users apparently understood it as something they could do: confess, pray, visualize, fast, copy their names into the figures, wait for visions, receive knowledge, interpret those visions, and continue the process. The Liber florum is therefore simultaneously a theological book, an autobiographical book, a ritual manual, a penitential program, and a technology for transforming the practitioner's relationship to knowledge.

That is the core of Fanger's argument about John's theological “language games”: John takes the grammar of sacramental Christianity and uses it to solve the problem posed by magic. He does not abandon the desire for extraordinary knowledge. He attempts to change the mode of mediation through which that knowledge is obtained—from suspect magical operations to confession, penitence, Marian intercession, prayer, visionary discernment, Scripture, and ultimately grace.

fanger discusses Morigny's daily habits what do we gather about them and how do they fit into her historiographical insights

Yes. Fanger gives us a surprisingly concrete picture of Morigny's routine, although it has to be reconstructed from scattered autobiographical and procedural passages rather than from anything resembling a diary. The important point is that she is interested precisely in this reconstruction: John's extraordinary visionary experiences are embedded in mundane, repeated bodily and institutional practices.

Fanger explicitly says that the “substrate” of John's knowledge was the Latin theological tradition as it functioned as his habitual daily knowledge. She contrasts John's lettered practices—canon law, liturgical composition, exegesis—with contemplative practices such as praying the canonical hours, working with ars notoria figures, and conversing with the Virgin. All of these, she says, rest on daily habits.

The basic shape of John's day

The clearest reconstruction looks something like this.

John was a Benedictine monk and, after 1308, provost of Morigny, responsible for practical administration involving rents and tithes. This is important because it prevents us from imagining him as a cloistered visionary whose entire existence consisted of mystical contemplation. He had an institutional job and occupied a position of considerable trust within the monastery.

At the same time, he was a highly literate professional intellectual. His habitual activities included reading and composing texts, legal study, exegesis, liturgical composition, and prayer. Fanger specifically identifies canon law as one of his “lettered practices” and praying the hours as one of his contemplative practices.

So the ordinary day should not be divided into “normal monastic life” and “magic.” The activities that look magical to us were inserted into an already highly ritualized monastic schedule.

Prayer was distributed through the day. The canonical hours provided a habitual rhythm of liturgical prayer. John's own ritual system added further prayer, often directed toward the Virgin and accompanied by meditation on particular images or figures.

The important distinction is that his visionary practice wasn't generally an isolated nighttime séance. It was built out of a cycle:

prayer → meditation → bodily preparation → sleep → dream/vision → waking recollection → interpretation → new prayer → further practice.

Fanger's 2018 article makes this especially explicit. Physical habits designed to solicit dreams included prayerful meditation, mild fasting, and scripted prayer before sleep. But she insists that these were only “half the story.” The other half was the continuing practice of reflecting upon and interpreting what had been seen.

Sleep was an active religious practice

This is probably the most revealing aspect of John's daily life.

John's visions normally occur at night, in dreams or states close to sleep. But Fanger does not treat “sleep” as the point at which his religious activity stopped. He deliberately prepared himself for sleep.

The practitioner might pray, meditate, fast mildly, perform a scripted prayer, and then go to sleep expecting—or at least hoping—that the Virgin or another divine intermediary might answer a question.

That means sleep becomes a ritual threshold.

The interesting thing is that John doesn't think the ritual mechanically causes the vision. God can refuse to answer. Fanger emphasizes that John's solicited visions remain genuinely open-ended: a person can establish long-term habits of praying and waiting for God, but God retains the freedom to answer, delay, or say no.

This is central to Fanger's rejection of the simplistic distinction between “magic” and “religion.” John's ritual is highly procedural, but its procedure is designed around divine freedom rather than mechanical control.

Fasting

Fasting appears as part of the physical discipline surrounding visionary practice.

Fanger characterizes it specifically as mild fasting, rather than implying extreme asceticism.

That qualification matters. John's visionary technology belongs to the same family of bodily disciplines found in Christian contemplative practice. The body has to be placed in a condition conducive to receptivity.

Fanger's larger comparative argument is that medieval people could understand bodily condition as part of cognition. Hunger, sleep, bodily balance, moral purity, prayer, and attention could affect the capacity to receive knowledge. Her comparison with Hildegard, Picatrix, and Jewish dream-prayer traditions is designed to show that this was a broad medieval “moral ecology” rather than an eccentric belief peculiar to John.

The Virgin was part of John's habitual mental world

John's relationship with Mary is not adequately described as occasional devotion.

He repeatedly prays to her, imagines her presence, speaks with her in visions, asks her questions, and composes new prayers in response to visionary encounters.

The important feedback loop is:

vision of Mary → composition of prayer → repeated prayer → further vision → reinterpretation → revised prayer.

Fanger emphasizes that John's prayers sometimes incorporate the content of earlier visions. The vision is theologized through Scripture and poetry and then transformed into a new prayer that can be used again.

This makes John's daily religious practice unusually recursive. He isn't simply performing a fixed liturgy. His experiences continually generate additions to the liturgy.

That is one reason Fanger describes the Liber florum as a dynamic project rather than a finished book.

Writing was itself a daily practice of transformation

Fanger makes a fascinating methodological analogy here.

She compares John's composition of his book to the scholarly practice of writing a modern academic book. Writing is a repeated practice embedded in a community's existing knowledge system. The writer doesn't merely produce an object; the act of writing changes the writer and potentially changes the knowledge system itself.

That is precisely how she thinks John's book developed.

John has a vision.

He interprets it.

He writes a prayer about it.

The prayer is incorporated into a larger ritual.

The ritual produces another vision.

The new vision causes him to reconsider the previous material.

He writes again.

Eventually criticism from other theologians forces another revision.

So John's “daily habits” include writing as a form of religious practice.

This is why Fanger doesn't treat the different versions of the Liber florum as merely editorial variants. They record changes in John's religious practice and theological understanding.

Confession and examination of conscience

Confession is another habitual structure.

John repeatedly interprets his life through penitential categories. His conversion from the ars notoria is followed by confession. His later encounters with necromancy similarly lead into repentance and reconsideration.

But confession is more than something he does after committing a particular sin. Fanger's larger argument is that penance provides the grammatical structure through which John understands the entire development of his knowledge.

John is continually asking:

What did I think I knew?

How did I acquire that knowledge?

Was the source legitimate?

What happened to me because of it?

What does God now require of me?

How should my knowledge be reconstructed?

That is why Fanger calls penance a “sacrament of the middle of life.” It provides a mechanism for repeated correction rather than a single conversion event.

John as administrator, teacher, and practitioner

The daily-life evidence also complicates the image of John as solitary.

He teaches his sister Bridget.

He teaches John of Fontainejean.

He corresponds with or participates in a circle of users.

He administers Morigny.

He interacts with colleagues who know about his visions.

He worries about what his community thinks.

And eventually people mock him as Sompniator, “dreamer,” perhaps with the implication of magician.

The result is a picture of a man whose visionary practice is socially embedded.

This becomes particularly significant when Bridget begins experiencing demonic phenomena after John teaches her the ars notoria. His magical experimentation is not merely private experimentation. He creates consequences for people around him.

What Fanger is really doing with these habits

This is where the daily-life material connects directly to her historiography.

Fanger's methodological target is the historian's tendency to separate categories that medieval actors did not necessarily separate.

We might classify:

prayer as religion,

fasting as asceticism,

sleep as biology,

dream interpretation as mysticism,

canon law as intellectual history,

book production as scholarship,

administration as institutional history,

and magical figures as magic.

Fanger argues that these are actually interlocking practices within John's life.

Her Wittgensteinian vocabulary is useful here. John is participating in multiple “language games,” but they are not sealed compartments. Canon law, liturgy, sacramental theology, biblical exegesis, magical ritual, and visionary practice supply him with overlapping rules for describing and evaluating experience.

And the bodily routines are what make those language games real.

John doesn't merely believe that God can answer dreams. He develops a practice of preparing himself to dream.

He doesn't merely believe in penitence. He repeatedly confesses and rewrites his understanding of his past.

He doesn't merely believe that Mary can mediate knowledge. He prays to her, visualizes her, sleeps expecting her response, records the response, and turns the response into another prayer.

He doesn't merely believe that knowledge can transform the soul. He designs a ritual system intended to make knowledge itself a means of resisting sin. Fanger explicitly points out that John's request for comprehensive knowledge is joined to his request for purification: knowledge is supposed to enable him to restrain himself from the “impious way.”

The really significant historiographical implication

Fanger's John is therefore neither “a magician who happened to be a monk” nor “a monk who briefly experimented with magic.”

He is a monastic knowledge practitioner whose magical experiment cannot be separated from his ordinary religious formation.

That is the larger historiographical intervention.

Older histories of magic tended to extract texts from their institutional and devotional settings and classify them according to modern categories: magic, mysticism, theology, superstition, science.

Fanger instead asks what happens if we reconstruct the practices of knowing that connected those categories for the historical actor.

John's daily routine is therefore evidence for a broader claim about medieval intellectual history: extraordinary experiences were cultivated through ordinary disciplines. Visionary knowledge was produced through habits of prayer, fasting, reading, meditation, sleep, confession, writing, and interpretation.

And this explains why Fanger is so interested in the phrase “habitual daily knowledge.”

The extraordinary thing about John is not that he occasionally stepped outside ordinary monastic life to practice magic. It is that he tried to make the extraordinary acquisition of knowledge into an extension of ordinary monastic life.

That is also why her later “Divine Dreamwork” essay is so important. She explicitly describes her research as an investigation of the “techniques and physical habits” used to bring about visions and the corresponding “habits of thought and interpretation” through which those visions were processed.

In other words, Fanger's historical John is best understood not through the question “What did John believe?” but through “What did John repeatedly do in order to become the sort of person who could receive, interpret, and act upon divine knowledge?”

That shift—from beliefs to practices, and from isolated texts to habitual forms of life—is probably the single most important historiographical insight we can extract from Fanger's treatment of his daily life.
