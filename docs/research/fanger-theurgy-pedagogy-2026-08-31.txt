what is surprising about John of Morigny's use of the ars notoria according to claire fanger? Contrast it with other knowledge we have of the ars notoria and its medieval magical uses, and the picture of the clerical necromantic underworld of Richard Kiekhefer

The surprising thing about John of Morigny’s Ars Notoria is not simply that a monk used a magical book. It is that, in Fanger’s reconstruction, John treats the Ars Notoria as something much closer to a devotional technology of education, literacy, and visionary inquiry than to what we would normally imagine as “necromancy”—and he uses it in ordinary intimate relationships, including teaching his fifteen-year-old sister to read.

That becomes especially striking when set against the broader history of the Ars Notoria and Kieckhefer’s “clerical underworld.”

1. What is unusual about John’s use?

The standard image of the Ars Notoria is a specialized grimoire for spectacularly accelerated learning. It promised access to the liberal arts, philosophy, theology, memory, and related subjects through prayers, fasting, contemplation of notae, and communication with angels. Fanger emphasizes that this was not simply a lazy “magic shortcut”: it imposed a substantial devotional regimen and could look surprisingly similar to monastic spiritual practice.

John's initial encounter with it therefore makes perfect sense. He was a young canon-law student at Orléans; the book was circulating in universities; he obtained his copy through the university library; and a physician from Bologna presented it to him specifically as a holy alternative to acquiring knowledge through nigromancia.

But then John does something particularly revealing: he turns the Ars Notoria into a pedagogical instrument for someone who is not a university student at all.

His younger sister Bridget repeatedly asks him to teach her to read. John initially thinks fifteen is too old to become a good reader, but eventually decides to put her through the Ars Notoria's learning process. He teaches her the letters, and within six months she is reading and writing; he then boasts that she can sing an Alleluia in church without assistance.

That is extraordinary evidence for how John actually conceptualized the thing. He isn't merely thinking:

“This grimoire will give me magical mastery of the university curriculum.”

He thinks, essentially:

“This is a technology for making a person capable of learning.”

And Fanger points out why this was not necessarily bizarre in its medieval context. Medieval children commonly learned literacy through prayer books, missals, antiphonals, and the Hours of the Virgin. The Ars Notoria's repetitive Latin prayers, familiar biblical language, and mnemonic structure could therefore look like an unusually powerful version of a perfectly recognizable pedagogical technology.

There is consequently something almost domestic about John's magic. The Ars Notoria migrates from the university into the household.

2. And then it becomes a technology of dreaming

The other surprising feature is that John discovers that the Ars Notoria doesn't merely give knowledge. It generates experiences that have to be interpreted.

Fanger's 2018 article makes this central. The Ars Notoria contains prayers and figures for accelerated learning, but its prayers can also generate visions of heavenly entities. John uses it repeatedly to solicit visionary dreams, including dreams intended to answer questions or interpret previous dreams.

This means that the Ars Notoria, in John's experience, becomes a kind of feedback system:

prayer → purification → figure/visualization → dream → knowledge → interpretation → new prayer/text → new dream

The Ars Notoria already contains something like this mechanism. Its preliminary prayers can produce an “inception” dream in which an angel tells the operator whether he is worthy to proceed. Later, if the operator makes an error, falls asleep, or encounters some difficulty, a dream can provide instructions about whether and how to continue.

John essentially takes this feature and makes it the engine of the Liber florum. Fanger argues that his Book of Prayers and the Ars Notoria operate in essentially the same practical way: they are systems of directed dreaming through which the operator seeks knowledge from the divine realm.

That is one of the really important implications for understanding John: he doesn't merely Christianize the content of the Ars Notoria. He understands its procedure as a method for entering into an ongoing epistemic relationship with the supernatural world.

3. But the Ars Notoria was already stranger than “a study aid”

This is where the broader medieval evidence complicates things.

The Ars Notoria itself occupies an unusual position in medieval learned magic. It presents itself as a divinely transmitted art, associated with Solomon, angels, and extraordinarily rapid acquisition of knowledge. Its basic operation is not the standard Solomonic pattern of constructing a circle, threatening a spirit, commanding it to appear, and extracting a service.

The Ars Notoria has prayers, mysterious names, figures, fasting, purification, contemplation, and angelic revelation. One of the sources you supplied explicitly emphasizes that it lacks the obvious apparatus of mainstream Solomonic evocation—circle, triangle, sword, censor, and so forth—and does not ordinarily issue straightforward commands to spirits.

And yet theologians had serious objections to it. By the time John encountered it, Thomas Aquinas had criticized it as unorthodox. The problem was not necessarily that the operator was obviously summoning demons. Rather, the Ars Notoria claimed access to supernatural knowledge through a ritual process that was not part of legitimate Christian mediation. Fanger summarizes the medieval theological objection as its being a form of theurgy: neither a properly covenanted mediation nor ordinary human learning, with an unintelligible language whose efficacy was difficult to account for.

That distinction is crucial.

The Ars Notoria was dangerous precisely because it looked holy.

Fanger makes this one of her strongest observations. Its Latin prayers could sound like extraordinarily elevated Christian liturgy, full of biblical language and theological petitions. Its objectives—knowledge, memory, understanding—were good. Its method involved prayer, fasting, contemplation, and angels. Nothing about its surface presentation necessarily announced “demonic magic.”

That is why John's story is so psychologically interesting. His problem is not that he knowingly chooses the obvious evil book. He chooses the beautiful, plausible, intellectually attractive book that seems almost too compatible with monastic devotion.

4. This is very different from Kieckhefer's necromantic world

Kieckhefer's “clerical underworld” provides a useful counter-image.

For Kieckhefer, later medieval necromancy normally means explicit demonic magic: conjuring demons, often by their names, in order to make them perform operations.

And the practitioners are precisely the people who possessed the educational and liturgical equipment necessary to do it: priests, clerics in minor orders, monks, friars, and university students. Basic familiarity with exorcistic formulae, Latin literacy, magical books, astrological images, and ritual procedure could provide the bridge from legitimate clerical learning into illicit conjuration. Kieckhefer calls this loose population a “clerical underworld,” while stressing that it wasn't an organized society.

The resulting magical world looks very different from John's Ars Notoria.

Kieckhefer's necromancers:

summon demons;
command or bargain with them;
manipulate people's minds and wills;
produce illusions;
seek hidden or future knowledge;
sometimes animate corpses;
employ circles, fumigations, astrological timing, magical images, sacrifices, and other ritual technologies.

The moral literature therefore gives us a familiar narrative: curious cleric encounters forbidden book → experiments with demons → suffers supernatural consequences → repents → seeks salvation.

John's story looks superficially like that story. And Fanger explicitly notes the resemblance to the conventional tales of sorcerers who are rescued from demonic magic by confession, repentance, and Mary.

But the Ars Notoria makes the story much stranger because the “forbidden book” doesn't look like the forbidden books in those exempla.

5. And there is an extraordinary overlap: necromancy could promise exactly the same thing

This is perhaps the most illuminating comparison.

Kieckhefer reports a necromantic experiment in the Munich handbook whose purpose is to summon a demon who will give the magician mastery of all the arts and sciences without effort.

So the difference between John's Ars Notoria and the necromantic underworld cannot simply be:

Ars Notoria = desire for knowledge
Necromancy = desire for practical magical power.

Both can promise supernatural acquisition of knowledge.

The difference lies in how the knowledge is mediated and how the operator understands the relationship with the supernatural.

The necromancer explicitly gets knowledge from a demon through conjuration.

The Ars Notoria says, in effect, that God and angels will open the human intellect through prayer, ritual purification, mysterious divine names, figures, and contemplation.

John initially finds that distinction entirely convincing.

And Fanger's great discovery is that his actual experience gradually destroys the distinction for him.

He does not see a demon being explicitly summoned by the Ars Notoria. Instead, after using the prayers, he starts having terrifying dreams in which demons appear. Yet he also has divine visions. The result is not simply “the Ars Notoria is demonic”; it is a bewildering mixture in which divine and demonic visionary experiences become entangled.

That is much closer to a phenomenology of magic than to the clean moral taxonomy of the exempla.

6. Bridget makes the distinction even more interesting

Bridget is arguably the most revealing piece of evidence.

John teaches her the Ars Notoria and she experiences spectacular success: literacy comes astonishingly quickly. Then the demonic visions begin. A malign spirit appears beside her bed, immobilizes her, threatens her, and terrifies her. Ordinary Christian prayers do not immediately drive it away. John eventually realizes that her suffering may be connected to the Ars Notoria, feels personally responsible because he taught it to her, and tells her to renounce the art before the Virgin.

This produces a very different social structure from the stereotypical necromancer.

The magical relationship is:

brother → teacher → sister → student

rather than:

magician → client → supernatural power.

Fanger's 2017 essay is explicitly interested in this. She uses John and Bridget to complicate the old sociological opposition between “religion as communal” and “magic as private.” John's magic is intensely private, but it is also embedded in obligations to family, friendship, education, and monastic community.

And there is a striking gender dimension: the Ars Notoria is not merely an elite male clerical technology in John's hands. He uses it to give literacy to his sister, apparently in anticipation of her religious vocation. Fanger notes that this was particularly intelligible because girls commonly learned to read through prayer books.

7. John is therefore both inside and outside Kieckhefer's “underworld”

I think this is the most productive way to put the two scholars together.

Kieckhefer shows us that the boundary between respectable clerical learning and illicit magic was extraordinarily porous. The same person could possess liturgical knowledge, exorcistic knowledge, astrology, magical books, and necromantic techniques. A university education could actually facilitate entry into the magical underworld.

John of Morigny fits that social ecology perfectly.

But his case exposes something that a category like “clerical necromancer” can obscure.

He doesn't necessarily experience his movement into ritual magic as a movement from religion → magic.

He experiences it as a movement from:

learning → devotion → supernatural knowledge → visionary experience → theological discernment.

The danger appears inside the devotional process.

That is why Fanger insists that the Ars Notoria should not simply be reduced to an ars magica promising miraculous results. For John, its attraction was that it looked like a disciplined form of devotion capable of reforming the self so that divine knowledge could become accessible.

8. The deepest contrast: Kieckhefer's demons are summoned; John's demons intrude

This is perhaps the cleanest conceptual distinction.

In Kieckhefer's necromantic material, the magician deliberately crosses the boundary. The ritual explicitly addresses supernatural beings, establishes control over them, and asks them to perform tasks. The ritual itself announces its illicitness.

In John's Ars Notoria experience, the operator is trying to cross the boundary in the opposite direction: he wants God to disclose knowledge to him. He fasts, prays, purifies himself, contemplates sacred forms, and waits for an angelic or divine revelation.

And then something unexpected happens:

the supernatural world answers—but John cannot reliably tell who is answering.

The Ars Notoria therefore becomes a machine for producing epistemological uncertainty.

That is why the visions of Bridget and John of Fontainejean are so important. John's friend asks the Ars Notoria for clarification and receives a vision whose message is effectively that the Ars Notoria itself is not pleasing to God. Yet, as Fanger observes, this is paradoxical: God appears to answer a question asked through a forbidden practice by telling the practitioner that the practice is forbidden.

That is very unlike the tidy moral universe of a necromantic exemplum.

9. And this changes how we should understand the Liber florum

John's eventual solution isn't simply to abandon everything he learned.

He rewrites the technology.

The Ars Notoria's basic epistemological ambition survives:

purify the operator → contemplate sacred forms → pray → dream → receive knowledge.

But John changes the supernatural economy. He puts Mary at the center, replaces the problematic magical apparatus with Christian prayer and visualization, integrates the process with confession and penitence, and eventually constructs his own figures and prayers.

Fanger therefore describes the Liber florum as having essentially the same practical structure as the Ars Notoria while radically reconceiving its theology.

And this is why the apparent opposition

Ars Notoria vs. Christian devotion

doesn't quite work.

John's mature project is better represented as:

Ars Notoria → crisis → discernment → Christian reconstruction of the Ars Notoria

rather than:

magic → repentance → abandonment of magic.

Indeed, John keeps the central promise of the condemned art: supernatural access to knowledge.

His Liber florum even preserves the Ars Notoria's conception of knowledge as something lost through the Fall and potentially restored through ritual purification. Fanger identifies this as one of the deeper continuities between the two traditions.

So the really surprising historical picture is not that John of Morigny was secretly a necromancer.

It is that the same clerical culture that could produce Kieckhefer's demon-conjuring necromancers could also produce John of Morigny: a monk who approached an occult grimoire as a devotional pedagogy, taught it to his sister as a literacy primer, used it to interrogate his dreams, encountered both demons and God through it, and then spent the rest of his intellectual life trying to determine what exactly had gone wrong—and whether the technology itself could be salvaged.

That makes John less an exception to Kieckhefer's clerical underworld than a particularly revealing case at its borderlands, where liturgy, pedagogy, visionary mysticism, angelic theurgy, and demonic magic were not yet cleanly separable categories.

Fanger's comparison with Hugh of St. Victor becomes much more interesting if we stop treating John as someone who merely “adds magic” to an otherwise conventional monastic theory of learning. Her argument is closer to: John inherits a recognizably Victorine theory of learning, but pushes several of its premises into a much more radical and experimental direction.

The basic continuity is quite strong.

Hugh's Didascalicon already treats learning as a form of moral and spiritual formation. Fanger emphasizes Hugh's insistence that the good student must be humble, docile, diligent, willing to learn from anyone, suspicious of pride, and concerned with becoming learned rather than merely appearing learned. Learning belongs within a larger discipline whose ultimate purpose is reformation of the self.

That gives us an important correction to the modern opposition between “academic learning” and “religious devotion.” For Hugh, the liberal arts are secular knowledge in one sense, but they are also prerequisites for theology and correct reading of Scripture. Learning is part of the movement of the soul toward God.

John is very much operating inside that model.

But Fanger identifies several ways in which he takes it further.

1. Hugh: learning reforms the student. John: learning can actually transform the structure of the knower.

This is probably the most important difference.

For Hugh, moral discipline is necessary for learning because pride, sensuality, distraction, and other defects interfere with intellectual formation. The student has to become humble and disciplined in order to learn properly.

John radicalizes that into something almost ontological: the right practices change the person's capacity to know.

Fanger says explicitly that for John the Ars Notoria's attraction was that it offered a targeted devotional discipline capable of the “reform of self” necessary for knowledge to be accessed and retained. Learning is an effect of grace, and its efficacy depends upon the disposition of the recipient.

This makes the Ars Notoria look less like a supernatural textbook and more like a technology for changing the epistemic subject.

So:

Hugh: discipline → properly formed student → better learning.

John: ritual/devotional discipline → transformed knower → access to knowledge that was previously inaccessible.

That is a significant escalation.

2. Hugh's student still learns through ordinary intellectual practices; John experiments with changing the medium of learning itself.

Hugh's model remains recognizably pedagogical: read, study, meditate, cultivate virtue, learn the liberal arts, and progress toward theology.

John's problem is that he encounters a text claiming that you can accomplish the same intellectual ascent through a different set of practices: prayer, fasting, figures, visualization, and angelic revelation.

Fanger thinks this is precisely why the Ars Notoria would have been attractive to a Benedictine intellectual. It did not necessarily look like an alternative to monastic learning. It looked like a way of intensifying monastic learning.

And this is why “devotional pedagogy” is such a useful phrase. John isn't simply asking God to give him facts. He is experimenting with whether devotional practice can itself function as an educational method.

3. Hugh has a fairly orderly curriculum; John turns the curriculum into a supernatural architecture.

This is where John's tables of knowledge become fascinating.

The Ars Notoria proceeds through the liberal arts toward philosophy and theology. John retains this curricular architecture, but his New Compilation creates a much stranger system in which the seven liberal arts are paired with:

the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit;
seven mechanical arts;
seven “exceptive” or magical arts;
and planetary/virtue correspondences.

For example, grammar is paired with necromancy and weaving, while other liberal arts are paired with other magical and mechanical disciplines.

This is not simply “magic added to the curriculum.” It implies that all these different kinds of knowledge participate in a common divine architecture of knowledge.

That is a major departure from Hugh.

Hugh can exclude magic from genuine knowledge. Fanger points out that in the Didascalicon, Hugh treats magic as false or unreal knowledge and thereby “excepts” it from the order of knowledge properly understood.

John instead creates the category artes exceptivae and actually gives these magical disciplines a place in his cosmic taxonomy.

That produces a fascinating ambiguity:

magic is excluded as a practice, but not necessarily excluded as knowledge.

John's prayer for necromancy says, essentially, “May I know and understand necromancy, but not perform it.” Fanger observes that this is a very peculiar compromise: speculative knowledge of magic is legitimate enough to request from God, even though its practical operation is prohibited.

That is something Hugh's cleaner taxonomy doesn't really permit.

4. Hugh's learning is fundamentally exegetical; John's becomes visionary.

This is another important shift.

Hugh's pedagogical system is deeply tied to reading. Scripture is God's writing in the world, and the liberal arts prepare the reader to understand it. Fanger describes De sacramentis as having a pedagogical purpose centered on spiritual reading and the interpretation of God's “writing” in the structure of things.

John inherits the assumption that the world is intelligible because it embodies divine structure.

But then he asks a different question:

What if God can teach me directly?

The Ars Notoria supplies precisely that possibility. Instead of merely reading God's signs in Scripture and creation, the practitioner can engage in a ritual that produces visionary communication.

This is why John's book is simultaneously a textbook, prayer book, autobiography, and record of revelations.

And it explains Fanger's claim that John's knowledge process involves knowledge initially external to the self—books or visions—being interiorized through reading, rereading, and practice.

John is therefore experimenting with vision as a mode of pedagogy.

5. Hugh's model is progressive; John's is recursive.

This is a subtler but very important difference.

Hugh gives you something like a ladder:

discipline → liberal arts → Scripture → theology → contemplation of God.

John's experience doesn't work that cleanly.

He learns something → has a vision → interprets the vision → writes a prayer → performs the prayer → has another vision → discovers that his previous practice was defective → rewrites the system → performs the new system → receives another revelation.

Knowledge changes the practice that produces knowledge.

This is why Fanger calls John's work a representation of its own knowledge process. The Liber florum doesn't merely contain information; it tries to reproduce the process by which John himself came to know.

That's much more experimental than Hugh's pedagogical treatises.

6. John also makes learning intensely experiential.

This is perhaps where Fanger's Wittgenstein/Bourdieu material becomes relevant.

She argues that John understands knowledge not as a static body of propositions but as something that exists “in practice”—a process occurring at the interface between the internal subject and the external communal world.

This helps explain why John's autobiography matters so much.

His dreams, errors, temptations, visions, prayers, revisions, and encounters with Mary are not merely colorful anecdotes surrounding his intellectual system. They are evidence of learning actually happening.

His autobiography is therefore itself a pedagogical artifact.

The reader is supposed to follow John's path rather than merely absorb John's conclusions.

7. And the endpoint of learning is not simply “knowing more.”

This is where John remains profoundly monastic.

Fanger says that John's devotional practices aim at reforming the entire self—“will, memory, and intellect”—into conformity with God. Reading and learning are part of this process.

That is very close to Hugh.

But John makes the relationship unusually symmetrical:

devotion teaches you, and learning makes you capable of devotion.

Fanger puts this particularly well when she says that “devotion was engaged with the aim of learning, and learning was engaged with the aim of devotion.”

That reciprocal structure is perhaps the best way to understand John.

8. John also democratizes the pedagogical system

Hugh is primarily writing for a learned religious/intellectual environment.

John's Liber florum is conceived as a lifelong devotional practice that can fit into monastic observance but can also be adapted for laypeople. Fanger says John had a pastoral interest in providing a prayer program that could be incorporated into regular devotional activity, with different levels of rigor according to the abilities of the operator.

And Bridget is the perfect concrete example.

The Ars Notoria is not only a university technology for someone studying theology.

John thinks it can teach a fifteen-year-old girl who has never learned to read.

That is a very revealing expansion of the Victorine educational project.

The really interesting historical trajectory

I would therefore put Fanger's genealogy something like this:

Hugh of St. Victor

Learning is a discipline of the whole person.
↓
The liberal arts reform the student.
↓
The liberal arts prepare the intellect to read Scripture.
↓
Reading reveals the divine structure of creation and redemption.
↓
Learning therefore participates in the soul's return to God.

John of Morigny

Learning is a discipline of the whole person.
↓
The liberal arts reveal a divine architecture of knowledge.
↓
Devotional practice can reform the knower's intellectual capacities.
↓
The Ars Notoria may therefore accelerate learning through devotional practice.
↓
Why not use it to teach literacy?
↓
Why not use it to ask questions of the supernatural world?
↓
Why not receive knowledge through visions?
↓
But if visions can teach, who is actually teaching?
↓
Demons appear.
↓
The original epistemology becomes suspect.
↓
John reconstructs the whole system around Mary, Christian prayer, sacramental theology, and discernment.

So I would actually revise the phrase from our previous discussion. “Devotional pedagogy” captures John better than “devotional technology” if we're comparing him with Hugh. John isn't simply importing a magical technique into monasticism. He is taking a very old monastic proposition—that disciplined learning transforms the soul—and asking whether the entire process can be made ritually operative, accelerated, visionary, and reproducible.

And that is also why Fanger's final comparison with Hugh is so consequential: by the fourteenth century, John can no longer maintain Hugh's relatively simple exclusion of magic from the order of knowledge. The proliferation of Arabic, Jewish, astrological, magical, and esoteric writings has made the boundaries of “knowledge” themselves unstable. Fanger explicitly situates John among late medieval scholars trying to decide which techniques and categories of knowledge could be formalized into Christian curricula and under what conditions.

In that sense, John of Morigny is not merely a monk who happens to practice magic. He is a late-medieval theorist of knowledge confronting the question of whether magic can be transformed into pedagogy—and whether pedagogy can become a form of spiritual transformation.

what about Fanger's invocation of the historiographical concept of theurgy as a term for approaching John's magic

Yes. This is one of the most important qualifications to the phrase “devotional pedagogy.” Fanger's use of “theurgy” is not simply a claim that John of Morigny was a medieval Neoplatonist practicing something called theurgy. It is a historiographical category that Fanger thinks can help us see what is distinctive about the mode of ritual action involved in the Ars notoria and John's rewriting of it.

The key is that she is deliberately asking us to hold three things apart:

what medieval practitioners themselves called what they were doing;
what medieval theologians thought they were doing;
what modern historians might call the practice in order to analyze its structure.
1. “Theurgy” is not John's own category in the straightforward sense

Fanger is very explicit about this in Invoking Angels. The term is Greek, originally meaning roughly “god-work,” and comes from late antique philosophical discussions, especially those surrounding Iamblichus. It described ritual activity involving the gods, as opposed to theologia, discussion or contemplation concerning the gods.

So there is an obvious historiographical danger: if we simply say “John practiced theurgy,” we can make a fourteenth-century Christian monk sound like a transplanted Iamblichean.

Fanger doesn't want to do that.

She notes that not all the contributors to Invoking Angels use “theurgy” in the same way and deliberately does not impose a single definition on them. The point is instead to make explicit the different ways scholars have found the category useful.

This is why she calls it a label or methodological tool.

2. Why is it useful?

Because “magic” carries a particular historiographical baggage.

Medievalists have often approached medieval ritual magic through nigromancy: explicit invocation of demons, coercion of spirits, illicit address to supernatural beings, and so forth.

But the Ars notoria doesn't behave much like a typical necromantic manual.

Fanger's discussion of Julien Véronèse makes the distinction very sharply. The practitioner of the Ars notoria doesn't primarily try to control God and the angels. The practitioner tries to attract their attention. Fanger's summary is essentially:

the operator adjures rather than conjures.

That distinction is enormously useful for understanding John.

A necromancer says, in effect:

“Spirit, I command you to appear and perform X.”

The notory practitioner says:

“Here is the divinely given sign/prayer/name through which I ask the heavenly powers to respond.”

The second model is structurally much closer to sacramental action or liturgical prayer.

And that is precisely why John could initially mistake the Ars notoria for a legitimate devotional practice.

3. Theurgy names an entirely different theory of ritual efficacy

This is perhaps the deepest reason Fanger likes the term.

In a coercive magical model, efficacy belongs principally to the magician:

I know the formula → I perform the ritual correctly → I compel the spirit → I get the result.

In theurgic logic, efficacy doesn't primarily reside in the operator. The ritual works because the signs, words, images, and divine powers have a relationship established by the divine order itself.

Fanger's discussion of Iamblichus emphasizes the idea that sacred prayers have been given to humanity by divinity and function as a kind of divine language. The ritual therefore “rouses” divine presence rather than forcing God to do something against his will.

That maps remarkably well onto John's own understanding of his practice.

It also fits the idea we were discussing earlier about devotional pedagogy: the operator's job isn't to become a magical superman. It is to become properly disposed to receive something.

4. But Fanger makes an important historiographical move beyond Véronèse

This is where her position becomes particularly interesting.

Véronèse uses “theurgy” partly because it lets him distinguish the Ars notoria from the demonological category of medieval magic. Fanger explains that he wants the term to separate this kind of ritual from the “demonological complex” constructed by medieval theologians around illicit address to supernatural intelligences.

Fanger says, essentially:

I agree that “theurgy” is useful, but not because it gets us out of the problem of magic and demonology.

She explicitly says that unlike Véronèse, she is interested in the term precisely because it engages the demonological problems associated with magic rather than escaping them.

That is a major distinction.

For Fanger, “theurgy” doesn't mean:

“This wasn't really magic; it was actually religious.”

It means something closer to:

“Here is a category that allows us to analyze a form of ritual action whose religious claims, mechanisms, and problems cannot be adequately captured by the category of demonic magic.”

5. And John of Morigny is almost the perfect test case for this

John's story demonstrates why the distinction matters.

He begins with nigromancy. He is contemplating explicit magical operations and is worried about the danger to his soul. Jacob of Bologna directs him toward the Ars notoria as the safe alternative: he can get knowledge without dealing with demons.

John therefore initially understands the universe in something like this way:

necromancy
→ demons
→ coercion
→ danger

versus

Ars notoria
→ angels/God
→ prayer
→ knowledge
→ safety

But his experience destroys the neat opposition.

He performs the supposedly safe ritual and encounters demons.

That is where Fanger's historiographical use of “theurgy” becomes especially powerful. The category doesn't tell us, “John was actually doing good Christian magic.” It helps us ask:

What kind of supernatural relationship did John think he was establishing, and why could that relationship become demonologically dangerous despite its devotional form?

6. The sacramental analogy is therefore crucial

Fanger sees a strong structural affinity between theurgic and sacramental reasoning.

The Ars notoria itself describes its mysterious prayers as “sacramental.” Its efficacy comes from divinely instituted ritual symbolism rather than the operator's personal magical power.

And Fanger points out that this wasn't merely a modern historian's analogy. Medieval thinkers themselves made the comparison.

Peter of Abano explicitly associated the Ars notoria with sacramental action, whereas Aquinas rejected that comparison, insisting that the Ars notoria was not divinely instituted and did not function like the Church's sacraments.

John then takes this sacramental logic even further.

Fanger says that the Liber florum brings sacramental theology into the very structure of the new ritual through its ideas of covenant and divine-human cooperation.

So John's reconstruction isn't merely:

bad magic → good magic.

It's:

condemned magical ritual → reinterpretation of ritual efficacy → sacramental/theurgic model of divine-human cooperation → Marian liturgy.

7. This also explains why “theurgy” is better than “angel magic” for some questions

Fanger has used “angel magic” as a description of texts like the Ars notoria, and she continues to find that useful. But “angel magic” primarily tells us what supernatural beings are being approached.

“Theurgy” tells us something about how the relationship is supposed to work.

That's a much more interesting distinction for John.

“Angel magic”:

John petitions angels for knowledge.

“Theurgy”:

John performs divinely sanctioned ritual signs intended to establish or intensify a relationship between the human subject and the divine/intelligible realm.

“Devotional pedagogy”:

That relationship is itself supposed to transform the practitioner's capacity for knowing.

Those three descriptions operate at different levels, and Fanger is effectively interested in all three.

8. There is also a fascinating irony in the medieval history

The category of theurgy lets us see why Christian ritual magic could be more troubling, not less troubling, than straightforward necromancy.

The necromancer obviously knows he is addressing a supernatural intelligence outside ordinary Christian mediation.

The theurgic practitioner can believe:

“I am not manipulating supernatural powers. I am participating in God's own divinely established system of mediation.”

That makes the practice look much more like sacramental theology.

But from Augustine's or Aquinas's perspective, that could actually be the problem. If God has not instituted this particular ritual, then the practitioner's claim that mysterious words, figures, or names have divinely guaranteed efficacy is itself suspect.

So the fundamental question becomes:

Who authorized the ritual?

That is the question underneath the distinction between sacrament and theurgy.

And it is precisely why John eventually needs the Virgin to authorize his replacement for the Ars notoria. His entire solution to the problem is effectively to establish a legitimate divine authorization for his new ritual.

9. Fanger's historiographical position is therefore quite sophisticated

I would formulate it this way:

“Theurgy” is not a synonym for “good magic.”

It is a comparative analytical category for a type of ritual whose practitioners understand themselves as participating in, or activating, a divinely ordered relationship between human beings and supernatural powers.

That category is useful for John because his magic is:

non-coercive in its self-understanding;
heavily dependent on prayer;
concerned with divine or angelic instruction;
dependent on ritual signs and sacred language;
oriented toward transformation and knowledge rather than material manipulation;
explicitly connected by its practitioners to sacramental efficacy;
yet condemned by theologians precisely because those claims to divine efficacy could be false.

And Fanger's most important caution is that we shouldn't let our historiographical category replace the medieval categories. “Theurgy” is something the historian uses to illuminate a problem; it is not necessarily the vocabulary through which John himself understood his practice.

That makes it especially useful for your comparison with Kieckhefer. Kieckhefer's clerical underworld gives you a powerful model of addressative magic as demonic negotiation and coercion. Fanger's theurgical category opens up the adjacent territory where the practitioner isn't trying to dominate spirits but to establish a ritually mediated relationship with the divine. John sits directly on the unstable boundary between those two models—and his autobiography is essentially the record of discovering that the boundary is much harder to police than he initially thought.

That, I think, is one of the deepest reasons Fanger's historiographical intervention matters: she is not trying to rescue John from the category of magic. She is trying to make “magic” capacious enough to include the very different theories of supernatural efficacy that medieval ritual texts actually contain.

give me a summary of theurgy in Fanger's Invoking Angels (attached as pdf)

Fanger's Invoking Angels: Theurgic Ideas and Practices, Thirteenth to Sixteenth Centuries is fundamentally a history of a problem of classification: what do we call ritual practices that seek direct contact with divine or angelic powers but present themselves as radically different from demonic magic?

For Fanger, “theurgy” is useful precisely because neither “magic” nor “religion” adequately captures this territory. She uses the term comparatively and historiographically, not simply as a claim that medieval Christians consciously thought of themselves as practitioners of late-antique Neoplatonic theurgy.

1. What Fanger means by “theurgy”

The starting point is late antique Neoplatonism, especially Iamblichus. Theurgy involves ritual practices through which human beings establish contact with divine powers. The crucial distinction from a crude conception of magic is that the practitioner does not necessarily command or coerce the gods.

Instead, divine power is activated through divinely established signs, words, images, prayers, and ritual actions.

Fanger is particularly interested in this theory of operative symbolism: ritual signs work because they participate in a divinely ordered symbolic system. The practitioner is not a magician possessing autonomous power.

This is why she finds the analogy between theurgy and Christian sacramentalism historically interesting. Both can involve the idea that an authorized ritual symbol makes divine power present or available. But Fanger cautions against reducing theurgy to some abstract essence such as “ritual that makes God present.” The theological systems that justify these rituals matter.

2. The Christian West develops its own form of theurgical practice

Fanger's central historical argument is that ideas structurally comparable to ancient theurgy appear in medieval Christian ritual texts.

The Ars notoria is the crucial case.

Its prayers combine ordinary Latin Christian liturgical language with mysterious words in Hebrew, Greek, Chaldean, and other languages. The text claims that these prayers possess extraordinary efficacy because they are divinely transmitted.

This makes the Ars notoria particularly interesting as a Christian theurgy.

The operator isn't saying:

“I command an angel to give me knowledge.”

Instead:

“I perform the divinely transmitted prayer through which God and the angels may respond.”

Fanger's memorable distinction is that the practitioner “adjures more than conjures.”

That is the basic ritual logic underlying the category.

3. The mysterious language is essential

The strange languages and apparently meaningless words in the Ars notoria are not accidental decorations.

Fanger connects them to the late-antique theurgical conception of sacred language. In Iamblichus, divine beings understand a sacred language whose efficacy does not depend on ordinary human semantic comprehension.

The Ars notoria adopts something similar: its mysterious words are treated as divinely given signs whose efficacy does not depend upon the operator understanding their meaning.

This is one reason Fanger thinks the Ars notoria cannot simply be analyzed as a book of magical spells.

Its theory is closer to:

the right sign + the right ritual disposition + divine authorization → divine response

than:

magician's knowledge + correct formula → supernatural coercion.

4. This is where the sacrament analogy becomes unavoidable

The Ars notoria actually calls its prayers “sacramental mysteries.”

Fanger discusses the text's claim that its mysterious prayers possess a sacramental character and compares this to the Eucharist and other Christian sacraments.

The comparison is not merely modern.

Peter of Abano explicitly used sacramental language in defending the Ars notoria. Aquinas, however, rejected the comparison: the Ars notoria had not been divinely instituted and therefore could not claim the same status as the sacraments.

This produces the central medieval problem:

When does a divinely efficacious ritual count as a sacrament, and when is it magic?

There isn't an easy answer.

5. “Theurgy” therefore doesn't mean “non-magic”

This is probably Fanger's most important methodological point.

She explicitly says that she finds the term useful not because it escapes the demonological problems associated with “magic,” but because it engages them.

The Ars notoria itself insists that it is orthodox and distances itself from magic.

Theologians can respond:

No. Your ritual is not a sacrament; therefore your supernatural efficacy is suspect.

And once the ritual involves angels, mysterious languages, and supernatural knowledge, the possibility of demonic involvement becomes unavoidable.

So Fanger wants us to preserve the tension rather than resolve it by saying either:

“This was really Christian devotion.”

or

“This was really demonic magic.”

“Theurgy” names the contested middle.

6. The operator's humility is part of the theory

One of the most important contrasts Fanger draws is between the theurgical operator and the coercive magician.

The ritual practitioner of the Ars notoria is not supposed to become a master of the spirit world. The operator petitions, purifies, fasts, prays, contemplates, and waits.

Fanger stresses that the operator “begs for divine contact in all humility” rather than exercising absolute power over superior beings.

This is essential to understanding why medieval practitioners could plausibly regard such activities as compatible with Christian devotion.

The practitioner isn't claiming:

“I have magical power.”

He is claiming:

“God has established this means of communicating with humanity, and I am humbly participating in it.”

That is an enormously different theory of agency.

7. But the distinction from necromancy isn't stable

Fanger repeatedly brings this problem back to nigromancy.

The distinction between conjuration and adjuration is important:

conjuration tends toward commanding or constraining supernatural beings;
adjuration asks, appeals, or invokes through a relationship of obligation or sacred authority.

The Ars notoria generally operates in the second register.

But medieval theologians could regard both as illicit forms of address to supernatural powers.

This is where Fanger's work intersects directly with Kieckhefer. Medievalists have often constructed their understanding of magic primarily around the clearer category of nigromancy, where demons are explicitly invoked. Fanger argues that this has made the Ars notoria unusually difficult to categorize.

8. John of Morigny is the most important medieval case

Fanger regards John as exceptionally valuable because his Liber florum preserves an unusually detailed account of what actually happened when someone used the Ars notoria.

John initially encounters it as an alternative to necromancy. He wants knowledge, but doesn't want to risk his soul through explicit demonic magic. The Ars notoria appears to provide exactly the right solution: supernatural knowledge through angelic/divine rather than demonic mediation.

But John's experience produces visions and eventually a revelation that the prayers themselves have been corrupted by demonic insertions.

That makes John crucial to Fanger's larger argument.

He demonstrates that the distinction between theurgical and demonic ritual was not merely an abstract scholarly problem. It was an experiential problem for practitioners themselves.

John performs a ritual believing that it connects him with God and angels.

He receives supernatural responses.

Then he has to figure out whether those responses actually came from God.

9. John's solution is not simply to abandon theurgy

This is the especially interesting part.

John does not simply conclude:

“Magic is bad; stop doing magic.”

Instead, he reconstructs the Ars notoria.

The Liber florum retains much of its basic structure: prayer, sacred language, figures, angelic/divine communication, disciplined ritual practice, and the pursuit of knowledge.

But John changes its theological and ritual framework, especially by placing the Virgin Mary at the center.

Fanger therefore sees John's project as an example of the Christianization/reconfiguration of theurgical structures, rather than simply their rejection.

The same basic question remains:

How can a human being establish a legitimate ritual relationship with divine powers that produces knowledge and spiritual transformation?

John's answer is increasingly Marian and sacramental rather than Solomonic.

10. The historiographical payoff

This is why “theurgy” is such a productive term for Fanger.

It lets us see a continuum that gets obscured if we divide the medieval world into:

religion | magic | necromancy

Instead, Fanger gives us something more like:

liturgical prayer → sacramental ritual → theurgical practice → angelic invocation → ambiguous magic → demonic magic/nigromancy

with enormous overlap and disputed boundaries.

And those boundaries are not merely modern classifications. Medieval authors themselves were fighting over them.

The Ars notoria says:

This is sacred.

Peter of Abano says:

This can be understood sacramentally.

Aquinas says:

No—it isn't divinely instituted and therefore cannot claim sacramental efficacy.

John says, in effect:

Something about this works, but I have discovered that the system has been corrupted; therefore I need to reconstruct its legitimate form.

Fanger, as historian, says:

Rather than forcing this into either “religion” or “magic,” let's examine its theurgical structure and the historical arguments through which people tried to legitimate or condemn it.

The central thesis in one sentence

For Fanger, “theurgy” is a historiographical tool for describing medieval ritual practices that seek transformative contact with divine or angelic powers through divinely meaningful signs, prayers, and ritual actions—practices that resemble both Christian sacramentalism and magic, and whose very ambiguity made their legitimacy a persistent theological and practical problem.

And that makes John particularly important: John's Liber florum is almost a laboratory in which the theurgical problem gets worked out from inside the experience of a medieval practitioner. The question isn't simply whether his magic was “really” Christian or demonic. The question is how a Christian intellectual could construct a theory of ritual efficacy in which prayer, learning, vision, angelic communication, sacrament, and magic could initially appear to belong to one coherent system—and what happens when that coherence breaks down.
