# Michael D. Bailey

## Who they are
Professor of History at Iowa State University, teaching medieval history and, periodically, the history of magic and witchcraft. Founding/associate editor of the journal *Magic, Ritual, and Witchcraft*.

## Core works relevant to this game
- *Battling Demons: Witchcraft, Heresy, and Reform in the Late Middle Ages* (Penn State University Press, 2003 [verify]).
- *Magic and Superstition in Europe: A Concise History from Antiquity to the Present* (Rowman & Littlefield, 2007) — a widely used survey.
- ***Fearful Spirits, Reasoned Follies: The Boundaries of Superstition in Late Medieval Europe*** (Cornell University Press, 2013) — traces how university-trained theologians and clerics, using scholastic method, constructed and repeatedly re-drew the category of "superstition" between roughly 1300 and 1500.
- "The Meanings of Magic," *Magic, Ritual, and Witchcraft* 1.1 (2006) [verify exact pages] — a historiographical essay explicitly interrogating the field's own analytic categories.

## Their key intervention
Bailey's most important contribution for this project is historiographical rather than archival: he has argued directly against treating "magic," "religion," and "science" (or "natural philosophy") as clean, stable, mutually exclusive medieval categories that scholars can simply sort practices into. Instead, he shows that the boundary lines were themselves the *product* of ongoing scholastic argument — theologians and canonists were constantly renegotiating where licit devotion ended and illicit "superstition" began, and the categories shifted according to who was doing the classifying, when, and for what institutional purpose. *Fearful Spirits, Reasoned Follies* traces this boundary-work as a two-century intellectual project, not a fixed doctrine John would simply have looked up. This directly challenges any scholarship (including, in its cruder uses, Kieckhefer's) that treats "clerical magic" as a stable sociological category rather than a live, contested classification always under construction by the very people doing the classifying.

## Narrative & mechanical implications for MORIGNY
- Bailey's critique is the sharpest available scholarly tool for exactly the tension the design brief asked the synthesis to surface: where Kieckhefer's "clerical underworld" implies a fairly stable milieu, Bailey's work insists the magic/religion/science boundary was itself unstable and constantly re-argued — which actually *supports* this game's founding thesis (`STYLE_GUIDE.md`'s Scholarly Value #1: "the porous boundary... is the game's subject, not a verdict the game hands down") more directly than Kieckhefer's own framing does on its own.
- The examination scene at Paris (`src/engine/chronicle.js`'s `createExamination`/`answerQuestion`/`verdict`, "three questions from the period's real objections to the *ars notoria*") is precisely a scene of scholastic boundary-classification in Bailey's sense — the examiners are not applying a settled rule, they are doing the same live categorization work Bailey describes theologians doing across the whole period. A pencil note here could make that explicit: John's trial isn't the application of a fixed definition of "magic" but one instance of the ongoing definitional contest Bailey documents.
- His point that classification serves the classifier's institutional purposes is a useful lens for Sens ("ecclesiastical politics; where questions about a monk's book start becoming procedures," `WORLD_DESIGN.md`) — the archdeacon's clerk, Evrart, and similar figures are not neutral appliers of settled law but participants in an active, interested boundary-drawing process, which should inform how their dialogue is written when the "clerical underworld" side-content expands.
- The Radical Axis's disposition-based verdict logic (`verdict()`'s scorn/defend/submit thresholds) could, without changing any numbers, be narrated more explicitly in Bailey's terms: the "departed" outcome is not John breaking a fixed law but John refusing to accept the examiners' *authority to classify* at all — a stronger, more historically precise framing of what makes the Radical path radical.
- Honest limit: Bailey's own archival focus is mainly fifteenth-century witchcraft/superstition discourse (Nider, Jean Gerson, etc.), later than John by a century or more; his value to this project is primarily historiographical/methodological (how to think about the categories), not a direct source for John-era `attested` facts.
