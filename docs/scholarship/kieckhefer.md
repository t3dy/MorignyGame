# Richard Kieckhefer

## Who they are
American historian of medieval religion and magic. John Evans Professor Emeritus of Religious Studies and Professor of History at Northwestern University, where he taught from 1975. Past president of the American Society of Church History (1997) and of the Societas Magica (1995–2004); elected to the American Academy of Arts and Sciences (2006).

## Core works relevant to this game
- *European Witch Trials* (1976).
- *Repression of Heresy in Medieval Germany* (1979).
- ***Magic in the Middle Ages*** (Cambridge University Press, 1989; rev. ed. 2000/2014) — the survey that frames learned/clerical magic against popular and demonic magic, and the source of the "clerical underworld" phrase this project already credits him with (`STYLE_GUIDE.md`'s terminology sheet, `WORLD_DESIGN.md`'s Orléans description).
- ***Forbidden Rites: A Necromancer's Manual of the Fifteenth Century*** (Penn State/Sutton, 1997) — edition and study of a Munich necromantic handbook, a century after John; this project already correctly scopes it to the epilogue/apparatus layer only (`BIBLIOGRAPHY.md`).
- *Theology in Stone* (2004) — outside this project's scope.

## Their key intervention
Kieckhefer's founding move was to insist that late-medieval ritual magic was not a survival of folk paganism but a **clerical practice**: literate men — often lower clergy, students, sometimes monks — with access to liturgical Latin, exorcism formulas, and the technical apparatus of the Church, who repurposed that apparatus for necromantic and image-magic ends. The "clerical underworld" names this milieu: magic as a clerical subculture, sociologically continuous with (not opposed to) licit religious training. *Forbidden Rites* made the case concrete with a single manuscript's actual contents — conjurations, illusions, love magic, divination — read as evidence of a real practicing community rather than inquisitorial fantasy. Kieckhefer's other durable contribution is his taxonomy of magic's registers (natural/demonic/'common' magic; the porous line between exorcism and conjuration), which set the terms almost every scholar below has since revised, extended, or pushed against.

## Narrative & mechanical implications for MORIGNY
- The game already names and credits the "clerical underworld" phrase correctly (quoted, cited on first use per the terminology sheet). Keep doing that everywhere the phrase recurs in new Orléans/Sens/Paris content — it is a term of art, not house vocabulary.
- Kieckhefer's point that magic and clerical literacy are the *same* skill set, not opposed ones, is the deep justification for the scriptorium's shared recitation grammar (`src/engine/scriptorium.js` reusing `src/engine/recitation.js`'s distraction/hold-fast loop) — copying scripture and copying a *nota* are mechanically identical because historically they were the same craft turned to different ends.
- His taxonomy (natural/demonic/common magic) is a useful check on any future Orléans NPC writing: the "clerical underworld" content the designer wants to expand should differentiate registers of practice rather than presenting "magic" as one undifferentiated illicit category — this sharpens rather than contradicts anything currently built.
- *Forbidden Rites* is explicitly fifteenth-century and belongs only in the whitening epilogue (`DESIGN.md`'s Whitening Arc); nothing in the core 1280–1330 play window should borrow its specific necromantic content without the on-screen anachronism dating the project already requires.
- Tension worth flagging, not resolving here: Kieckhefer's own later work and the scholars below (esp. Bailey, Page) complicate the "clerical underworld" as a *separate* milieu from ordinary monastic life — which is close to this game's central thesis (the porous boundary). The synthesis document should take up where his frame gets revised rather than simply cited.
