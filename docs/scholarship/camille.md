# Michael Camille

## Who they are
(1958–2002) British-American art historian; Mary L. Block Professor of Art History at the University of Chicago from 1985 until his death. Pioneering scholar of medieval visual culture, marginalia, and the ideology of images.

## Core works relevant to this game
- ***Image on the Edge: The Margins of Medieval Art*** (Reaktion Books, 1992) — this game's bibliography already cites it as "warrant and grammar for the drollery-pressure mechanic."
- *The Gothic Idol: Ideology and Image-Making in Medieval Art* (Cambridge University Press, 1989) — on the theology and anxiety of image-making, relevant background to any content about the *ars notoria*'s figures as images with power.
- *Mirror in Parchment: The Luttrell Psalter and the Making of Medieval England* (Reaktion/University of Chicago Press, 1998) — a close single-manuscript study modeling how margin, text, and patronage interrelate.

## Their key intervention
Camille's central claim in *Image on the Edge* is that marginal drawings in medieval manuscripts — grotesques, hybrid creatures, scatological jokes, parodic inversions of the main text's authority — are not meaningless decorative overflow but a **structured commentary zone**, working in dialectical tension with the authoritative center of the page. Margins could mock, undercut, or complicate the text and image they bordered, licensed precisely by their marginal status (they were "outside" serious discourse, and so could say what the center could not) — while never actually overturning the center's authority. The margin is where a controlled, sanctioned form of transgression lives, in service of (not opposition to) the manuscript page's overall order.

## Narrative & mechanical implications for MORIGNY
- The game's recitation distraction mechanic (`src/engine/recitation.js`, described in `SLICE_SPEC.md` as "marginal glosses") is already explicitly modeled on this "Gothic margin" pressure, and `STYLE_GUIDE.md`'s binding rule that "the Gothic margins may be wry... they historically were" but "never comic at his expense" is a direct, correct application of Camille's finding that margins pressure and comment without ever fully undermining the center.
- Camille's point that the margin's power comes specifically from its *licensed* marginality — not from being unconstrained — suggests the distraction pool's design should keep leaning into structured, period-specific forms of marginal commentary (parody of a specific text, a specific figure of speech, a specific liturgical moment) rather than generic "intrusive thoughts," to stay true to what actually made medieval margins funny/threatening rather than merely distracting.
- The design brief's suggestion that "Camille's marginalia work and the game's existing 'Gothic margins' drollery-pressure mechanic could go further" is worth taking seriously: Camille's margins often specifically comment on or invert the *adjacent text's content* (a marginal image glossing the psalm above it ironically). The current distraction pool is content-agnostic (any distraction can appear regardless of what's being recited); a richer implementation could key specific drolleries to specific prayers/psalms being recited at that moment, which would be a genuine mechanical extension, not just flavor text — flagged here as a possibility for the designer to weigh, not implemented.
- *The Gothic Idol*'s theme (anxiety about images having power, images as potentially idolatrous) is directly relevant to the figure-drawing/*notae* mechanic (`src/engine/scriptorium.js`'s `drawFigure`) — the *ars notoria*'s figures are exactly the kind of image-with-power Camille's broader work is concerned with, and a pencil note connecting the figure mechanic to period image-anxiety (not just "wrong figure = void ritual" as a mechanical fact) could deepen that system's historical grounding.
- Honest limit: Camille does not, to this researcher's knowledge, write about the *ars notoria*, John of Morigny, or ritual magic manuscripts specifically. His value here is entirely about the *visual/marginal grammar* of medieval manuscript pages generally, transferred to this game's UI metaphor — not a source for any John-specific `attested` claim.
