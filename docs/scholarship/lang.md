# Benedek Láng

## Who they are
Historian of science specializing in late medieval manuscripts of learned magic and early modern secret communication (ciphers, artificial languages). Long based at the Department of Philosophy and History of Science, Budapest University of Technology and Economics (chair from 2013), and, per more recent listings, now affiliated with Eötvös Loránd University (ELTE) in Budapest [verify current institutional home].

## Core works relevant to this game
- ***Unlocked Books: Manuscripts of Learned Magic in the Medieval Libraries of Central Europe*** (Penn State University Press, 2008) — this game's bibliography already cites it for "witness/stemma flavor."
- *Real Life Cryptology: Ciphers and Secrets in Early Modern Hungary* (Amsterdam University Press, 2018 [verify]).
- *The Rohonc Code: Tracing a Historical Riddle* (Penn State University Press, 2021) — study of an undeciphered manuscript, with Teodóra Király.

## Their key intervention
*Unlocked Books* extends the manuscript-based approach of scholars like Klaassen and Láng's own doctoral advisors into Central Europe (Bohemia, Poland, Hungary), showing that learned magic texts circulated, were copied, owned, and occasionally condemned in university and monastic libraries across the region on a pattern recognizably similar to the Western European material — but with a distinct regional flavor of ownership (court physicians, university masters, monastic collectors) and a distinct manuscript afterlife shaped by later confessional and political history (many Central European magic manuscripts survive precisely because they were seized, catalogued, or preserved by institutions with an interest in tracking heterodoxy). His work is valuable for this project mainly as a case study in how a text's **manuscript census** — where copies ended up, who owned them, which survived — becomes its own historical argument, independent of the text's original content.

## Narrative & mechanical implications for MORIGNY
- Láng's manuscript-census method is the closest real-world analogue to what the stemma screen (`src/engine/stemma.js`) does in miniature: siglum assignment, tracking which witness descended from which, and treating "who owned this copy and where it ended up" as itself the historically interesting question — more than the text's content. The stemma's design language ("what got out, and how corrupt it was when it did," `SCRIPTORIUM.md` §3.7) is exactly Láng's kind of question asked about a single fictional run instead of a real regional corpus.
- His finding that texts survive *because* institutions had reasons to track and preserve heterodox material (not despite it) is a useful, slightly ironic counterpoint to the game's inventory/suspicion mechanics (`SCRIPTORIUM.md` §3.6, the planned Evrart inventory encounter F-5 in `docs/DECISIONS_AND_FORKS.md`) — being found by an inquisitorial-adjacent process is not always simple destruction; sometimes it is how a text gets catalogued into the historical record at all. This could sharpen the framing ending's irony (the manuscript survives via routes that also involve institutional suspicion) without contradicting rule 5's fixed-1323 constraint.
- *The Rohonc Code* — a study of an undeciphered manuscript — is a good comparative case for `verba ignota`'s design (`SLICE_SPEC.md`, `SCRIPTORIUM.md` §3.3): real manuscripts of ambiguous or possibly-invented content do exist and do generate genuine scholarly uncertainty, which is a useful register check on how the pencil hand talks about the game's own invented unintelligible text.
- Honest limit: Láng's primary geography (Bohemia, Poland, Hungary) does not intersect John of Morigny's northern France at all. His relevance here is entirely structural/methodological (how to think about manuscript survival and stemma-building), not a direct source for any Morigny-specific `attested` claim — content citing him should be careful to frame it that way.
