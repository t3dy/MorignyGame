# Katelyn Mesler

## Who they are
Historian of medieval Europe with a PhD in Religious Studies from Northwestern University. Research centers on Jewish-Christian relations, the history of science and medicine, magical beliefs and practices, apocalyptic thought, mysticism, and angelology. Has been affiliated with the University of Pennsylvania's Katz Center for Advanced Judaic Studies and, per more recent listings, the Hochschule für Jüdische Studien (HfJS) in Heidelberg; also lists as an independent researcher in some records. ## Their key intervention
Mesler's work sits precisely at the intersection the design brief flags as underexplored in this project so far: **cross-cultural (Jewish-Christian) transmission of magical texts and reputations**. Her dissertation work on "legends of Jewish sorcery" examines how a reputation for magical/occult knowledge attached to Jewish communities and individuals in Christian sources — not necessarily reflecting actual practice, but functioning as a discourse Christian writers used to explain the origin or authority of certain magical or scientific knowledge (the trope of magic/science as secretly "borrowed" from Jewish or Solomonic sources). *Entangled Histories* more broadly models how knowledge, authority, and textual culture crossed the Jewish-Christian boundary in the thirteenth century — the exact period John was born into — via translation, appropriation, and contested claims of origin.

## Narrative & mechanical implications for MORIGNY
- The *ars notoria*'s own claimed genealogy (a Solomonic art, i.e., claiming Hebrew/biblical royal authorship) is exactly the kind of cross-cultural authority-claim Mesler's work on "legends of Jewish sorcery" would help contextualize — currently the game treats "Solomonic" as flavor description (`DESIGN.md` calls the *ars notoria* "a Solomonic ritual art") without engaging the actual cross-cultural discourse behind that claim of origin. This is a genuine gap her work could fill if the design wants to go deeper here, though it is presently unbuilt.
- The *verba ignota* — the "unknown Hebrew/Greek/Chaldean words" in the *ars notoria*'s prayers (`SCRIPTORIUM.md` §1) — are a direct instance of the phenomenon Mesler studies: Christian ritual-magic texts claiming exotic (often specifically Hebrew-coded) linguistic authority for their power. A pencil note connecting the *verba ignota* mechanic to this specific scholarly discourse (rather than leaving it as generic "unintelligible words") would be a precise, well-sourced deepening of an already-built mechanic.
- Her broader point that "magic" reputations often crossed communities as *discourse* (accusation, borrowed prestige, claimed origin) rather than as verified shared practice is a useful caution against ever depicting actual Jewish characters, practices, or texts in-game without direct, specific sourcing — the risk of reproducing exactly the kind of unverified attribution her scholarship studies and critiques is real, and any future content in this space should be handled with particular care and probably direct scholarly consultation beyond this report.
- Honest limit: this researcher could not confirm Mesler has written specifically about the *ars notoria* or John of Morigny; her relevance here is thematic and structural (the cross-cultural transmission question the design brief explicitly asked about), assembled from adjacent published work, not a direct source for any John-specific claim. Treat everything above as a starting point for further reading, not a settled citation.
