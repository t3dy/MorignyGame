MORIGNY

Liber Florum Celestis Doctrine — a monastic life & practice simulator

John of Morigny was a real man: a Benedictine of the abbey of Morigny near Étampes, a priest, a canon lawyer trained at Orléans, and after 1308 his house's provost. He was also, for years, a practitioner of forbidden arts — first a necromantic book he copied from a cleric, then the ars notoria, which promised the whole of human learning to anyone who could keep its prayers and fasts. He came to believe the art had been contaminated by demons. He did not stop. He rewrote it, as the Liber florum celestis doctrine, and claimed the Virgin herself had authorized the rewriting.

In 1323 a chronicle records that a monk of Morigny's book was condemned and burned at Paris. It survived anyway, in copies, in houses he never saw. This is a simulation of that life, built on Claire Fanger's scholarship and answerable to it: every line of writing carries a source and a status — attested, adapted, or invented — and the game will show you which.

The four voices

Every moment the game stops and waits for you, it owes you four things. This is checked mechanically — a branch that fails to orient, voice, ground, or instruct fails the build.

The narrator

Third person, modern, analytical. Explains the monastic and magical world and expects no prior knowledge.

John's hand

His own words, in the idiom of his century — never clinical, never modern, whatever the narrator just said plainly.

The pencil hand

The designer-scholar: what is attested, what we invented, and where the uncertainty sits.

The ledger

What the machine actually did. It never discloses a silent failure — that honesty lives at the reckoning.

The research behind it

Citations are machine-checked. Every source a line of writing claims must resolve to a registry that records what kind of claim it is — a primary text, modern scholarship, standard period practice not yet pinned to a page, or our own design reasoning. Summaries of scholarship are finding aids and may never be cited as authorities.

  • Claire Fanger, Rewriting Magic — the canonical interpretive source, and the model for the pencil hand
  • Fanger & Watson, the critical edition of the Liber florum
  • Sophie Page, Magic in the Cloister — the monastery as a production environment for magic
  • Richard Kieckhefer, Magic in the Middle Ages — the "clerical underworld," credited on first use
  • Julien Véronèse on the ars notoria; Michael Bailey on boundary-work; Edward Peters on accusation documents
  • …and fourteen more, each with a brief in the repository
A correction worth naming. This project spent months simulating John's central struggle as sexual temptation, citing Fanger for it. On reading her argument carefully, that is close to the opposite of what she documents: the desire for forbidden knowledge is the temptation, and John asks God for comprehensive knowledge precisely so that he may resist sin. The system was retargeted, the citation was corrected in place rather than quietly replaced, and a research pipeline now makes such claims checkable. The account is kept in the repository.
